Thread · Great-power realignment

The Ukraine war is testing how far Washington, Kyiv and Europe still pull together

Where it comes from. The arc began with Russia's full-scale invasion in February 2022. Two years of attrition followed, and in 2025 and 2026 Europe began debating its own deterrence while the parties probed for a ceasefire. The open question now is who carries the cost of an unsettled war.

What is driving it underneath. War fatigue and burden-sharing drive this thread. The US administration wants a negotiated pause and less exposure. Russia holds to maximal territorial demands, and Ukraine depends on Western systems it does not control.

Who is driving it, and toward what.

  • Trump administration
    Aim: Washington says it wants a negotiated end to the fighting. Its actions match that aim in part: it brokered the Oleshky truce and new talks while criticising Kyiv's strikes on Russian energy sites.
    Evidence: US-led talks stalled in February, and a new round is planned for next month. Trump has openly criticised Zelensky over strikes on Russian energy sites.
    Says vs does: The administration presents itself as a neutral broker, but its public pressure has fallen mainly on Kyiv's strikes rather than on Russian attacks. E7 E14
  • Russian government
    Aim: Moscow says it seeks peace, yet its demand for all of Donbas, including land it does not hold, keeps maximal territorial goals on the table.
    Evidence: Russia's demand was reported alongside the Oleshky agreement.
    Says vs does: Russia agreed a local truce while maintaining a demand for territory it has not taken, so its stated interest in peace and its negotiating terms diverge. E7
  • Zelensky government
    Aim: Kyiv says it wants a just peace and keeps striking Russian energy infrastructure to raise the cost of the war for Moscow.
    Evidence: The energy-site strikes triggered the rift with Trump. Kyiv also disputed Musk's account of how it used Starlink. E14 E10

What's at stake.

  • Ukraine: Ukraine stands to keep or lose sovereignty over occupied and contested land, and its security depends on Western weapons and intelligence it does not control.
  • Russia: Russia seeks territorial gains written into a settlement and relief from sanctions, while its energy revenue is exposed to Ukrainian strikes.
  • United States: Washington weighs a smaller financial and military burden against its credibility with allies who see Russia as a lasting threat.
  • European governments: European capitals face higher defence spending and a direct security risk if a settlement leaves Russia stronger on their border.

The tensions.

  • Sovereignty vs ceasefire: A quick truce can freeze Russian gains, while holding out for full sovereignty prolongs the fighting and its costs.
  • Alliance cohesion vs domestic politics: US burden-shifting answers domestic pressure at home, but it strains trust with European allies and with Kyiv.

Follow the money.

  • Ukraine Support Loan allocations: EU institutions → Ukraine. About EUR 11bn in May-June 2026, roughly one-eighth of the EUR 90bn loan (2026-08-13) R5
  • Oil and gas taxes funding the war budget: Russian energy producers → Russian federal budget. RUB 5.47tn in January-September 2026, down 17% from a year earlier (2026-10-06) R6

What we don't know. The terms of the Oleshky ceasefire, including who monitors it, are not public. There is no public record of how much of the EU's committed loan money has reached Ukraine's military since June.

Competing readings. Supporters of the US approach call it realism: freeze the war and shift costs to Europe. Critics, including Kyiv's European backers, see pressure on the victim that rewards Russian demands. Ukraine calls its strikes on Russian energy legitimate self-defence, while the White House sees them as obstacles to talks.

How today's events connect.

  • The Oleshky truce happened because Washington is pushing for a deal before winter. It affects Kyiv, which must negotiate while Russia still demands all of Donbas.
  • Bulgaria's run-off is contested over the same war because voters are split on aid to Kyiv. The result affects whether another EU and NATO capital argues for dialogue with Moscow.

Current direction. The observable momentum is toward armed coexistence rather than a settlement. Local truces and talks now sit alongside continuing strikes, and European governments are carrying a growing share of the cost.

Counter-forces.

  • Britain and France
    Why it resists: Their joint exercise on defending European countries from a modern attack prepares for Russia as a lasting threat, not a negotiating partner.
  • Ukrainian government
    Why it resists: Kyiv keeps striking Russian energy targets so that it does not negotiate from weakness.

What's unresolved. It is unresolved whether Russia's demand for all of Donbas is a negotiating position or a fixed aim. It is also unresolved how far Washington's support for Kyiv depends on Ukraine accepting talks on those terms. E7 E14 E30

What is driving it underneath. Cost of living, distrust of elites and war fatigue drive this thread. Inflation has topped Ipsos's global worry list for 17 months. Median dissatisfaction with democracy in high-income countries is 64%.

Who is driving it, and toward what.

    Evidence: Progressive Bulgaria won 44.5% and 131 of 240 seats in April 2026. The party backs Yotova. G16 R1
  • Pro-Western Bulgarian opposition
    Aim: The opposition wants Bulgaria firmly aligned with Ukraine and the EU mainstream, and it backs Andrey Gyurov.
    Evidence: As caretaker premier, Gyurov signed a security agreement with Kyiv. R1
  • Trump administration
    Aim: The administration is contesting results it disputes. It sued to block certification of Alaska's Senate result if Sen. Dan Sullivan loses, and the Attorney General says this is not meddling.
    Evidence: Todd Blanche defended the lawsuit in public. E4
  • Democratic Party
    Aim: Democrats aim to win back Congress on the cost of living. Barack Obama campaigned for them in Michigan and Ohio.
    Evidence: US prices are up 5.4% and gas costs are climbing, according to the report on voter backlash. E5 E12

What's at stake.

  • Voters: Voters want relief from prices and corruption, and their trust in the institutions that count their votes is at stake.
  • Incumbents: Governing parties stand to lose power, as Radev did in Bulgaria and as the House majority may in the US.
  • Courts and election officials: Their authority rests on being seen as neutral while they settle disputed results.

The tensions.

  • Majority rule vs institutional checks: Winners claim a mandate, while courts and presidents with veto power hold them back.
  • Anti-corruption demands vs personal rule: Voters back outsiders to clean up politics, but some outsiders then concentrate power in their own hands.

What we don't know. Official Bulgarian first-round results were not final at publication; the shares shown come from an exit poll. The outcome of the Alaska certification lawsuit depends on a court that has not ruled.

Competing readings. One reading treats these votes as a healthy correction, with voters punishing incumbents over prices and corruption. Another sees institutions under strain, citing record-low turnout in Bulgaria and legal fights over certification in the US. Which reading fits differs by country.

How today's events connect.

  • Bulgaria's run-off happened because turnout fell below the 50% needed to win outright. The result affects whether Radev controls both the government and the presidency.
  • The Alaska lawsuit is driven by the fight for Senate control. It affects trust in certification at a time when 69% of Americans say they are dissatisfied with how democracy works.

Current direction. The observable momentum is toward close, low-trust contests in which losing sides challenge the process. Bulgaria's run-off on 2026-11-01 and the US midterms on 2026-11-03 are the next contests in that pattern.

Counter-forces.

  • Courts and election officials
    Why it resists: They decide certification disputes such as Alaska's.
  • Anti-corruption protest movements
    Why it resists: Bulgaria's Gen Z protests and Serbia's Student List push for accountability rather than personal rule.
