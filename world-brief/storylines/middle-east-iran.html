Middle East and the Iran axis

Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history inflection

  1. 1979-1989 Revolution, war, and forward defense

    The 1979 Islamic Revolution and the 1980-1988 Iran-Iraq War pushed Tehran and the IRGC toward forward defense after Iranian leaders concluded that conventional weakness on Iranian soil invited attack. By the late 1980s, Tehran had started using Lebanese Hezbollah and Iraqi Shi'a networks as external deterrents against Israel, Iraq, and the United States.

  2. 1990-2002 Hezbollah-centered consolidation

    During the 1990s, Hezbollah in Lebanon became Tehran's most disciplined external partner, while Iranian policy stayed pragmatic enough to work with Sunni and Christian actors when state interests required it. The model in 2002 still rested on a narrow Levant core rather than a region-wide expeditionary system.

  3. 2003-2014 Iraq and Syria expand the network

    The 2003 United States invasion of Iraq removed Tehran's main Arab buffer and gave Iran-linked Iraqi factions room to enter state institutions and armed formations. The 2011 Syrian war, the 2014 fight against ISIS, the growth of Iraq's Popular Mobilization Forces, and the 2014 Houthi takeover of Sanaa turned a Levant-centered posture into a corridor from Iraq and Syria to Lebanon and Yemen.

  4. 2015-2022 Mature but costly deterrence

    After the 2015 JCPOA, Tehran kept investing in missiles, drones, and armed partners because sanctions relief did not change the IRGC view that Israel and the United States still threatened regime survival. By 2022, the network offered Iran deniability and reach, but sanctions, local backlash, and divergent partner agendas were already eroding cohesion.

  5. 2023-2024 Gaza war activates every front and exposes limits

    The 7 October 2023 Hamas attack and Israel's Gaza war pulled Hezbollah, Iraqi factions, and the Houthis into coordinated pressure on Israel and United States positions from Lebanon, Iraq, Syria, and Yemen. By late 2024, Israel had badly damaged Hezbollah, the Houthis had launched more than 290 attacks involving at least 165 ships in the Red Sea campaign, and the December 2024 fall of Bashar al-Assad cut Tehran's Syrian land bridge.

  6. 2025-2026 From external shield to direct interstate war

    The June 2025 Israel-Iran war and the February 2026 United States-Israel strike on Iran ended the old assumption that Tehran could confine conflict to partners outside Iranian territory. By April 2026, ACLED counted more than 660 Iranian strike events against Gulf targets, and the June 2026 U.S.-Iran memorandum failed to settle sanctions, Hormuz access, or the nuclear file, leaving October 2026 with a degraded but still dangerous Iran-led network.

Where today sits. Inflection: October 2026 sits below Tehran’s pre-2023 corridor model, because Bashar al-Assad fell in December 2024 and Hezbollah lost commanders, legitimacy and border freedom in 2024. October 2026 also sits at the most dangerous point for direct interstate conflict since 1979, because Israel and the United States struck Iran in 2025 and February 2026 and ACLED counted more than 660 Iranian strike events against Gulf targets by April 2026. 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8

Trajectory and driving forces

Since October 2023, the Iran axis has been moving from an integrated deterrence system centered on Hezbollah, Syria, and deniable pressure toward a fragmented coalition in which the Houthis, Iraqi militias, and Palestinian fronts act with more local autonomy and less Iranian control. Since February 2026, Israel and the United States have treated Iranian reconstitution in Lebanon, Syria, Iraq, Gaza, the Red Sea, and the Gulf as grounds for repeated preemption, while Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, and Oman have tried to cap escalation because March and April 2026 attacks hit refineries, ports, power plants, and data centers. The most likely 2026-2027 path is repeated flare-ups across Gaza, the West Bank, Lebanon, Iraq, Yemen, and Gulf waters rather than a stable bargain, because neither Tehran nor Israel has accepted the other's minimum security terms and Washington wants open sea lanes more than another open-ended ground war. 1 2 3 4 5 6 7

  • IRGC survival logic. The IRGC still sees external armed partners as cheaper than rebuilding a conventional shield after the 1980-1988 war and after the 2026 strikes on Iranian territory. Tehran therefore keeps funding missiles, drones, and partner ties even when Hezbollah, Hamas, or Iraqi factions cannot deliver unified action.
  • Israeli denial strategy after 7 October. Israeli leaders after 7 October 2023 have aimed to prevent any reconstitution of cross-border strike capacity in Gaza, Lebanon, Syria, and Iran itself. That doctrine makes even partial rearmament by Hezbollah, Hamas, or IRGC-linked networks a trigger for raids, airstrikes, closures, and pre-election security moves.
  • Partner autonomy in Yemen and Iraq. Ansar Allah in Yemen and Popular Mobilization Force factions in Iraq do not act as simple extensions of Tehran, because each movement has domestic enemies, budget needs, and survival constraints inside Sanaa and Baghdad. That autonomy keeps the network alive after Iranian setbacks, but it also makes coordinated de-escalation harder for any single mediator in Muscat or Doha.
  • Gulf hedging and energy exposure. Saudi Arabia, the UAE, Qatar, Kuwait, Bahrain, and Oman want coexistence with Iran more than direct war, because March-April 2026 attacks showed how quickly refineries, LNG plants, desalination systems, and airports can be hit. Gulf caution restrains a unified anti-Iran coalition, but Gulf bases and energy sites still give Tehran and its partners attractive coercive targets.
  • Palestinian front as political fuel. Gaza deprivation and West Bank friction keep the Palestinian arena central for recruitment, legitimacy, and agenda setting from Beirut to Sanaa. Israel's military gains since 2023 have therefore weakened Iran's old network materially while preserving the issue that lets Tehran and armed partners justify continued confrontation.

Turning points

  1. 1979-1982

    Iranian Revolution and Hezbollah formation The 1979 overthrow of Mohammad Reza Shah and the 1982 Israeli invasion of Lebanon gave Tehran both an ideology of export and a disciplined armed partner on Israel's border. That pairing created the first durable version of Iran's forward-defense model. 1 2 3

  2. 2003

    United States invades Iraq The 2003 fall of Saddam Hussein removed Iran's main western buffer and let Iran-linked Iraqi groups enter politics, ministries, and armed formations. Iraq then became the central land route between Iran, Syria, and Lebanon. 1 2 3

  3. 2011-2014

    Syrian war, anti-ISIS mobilization, PMF rise, and Houthi takeover of Sanaa The 2011 Syrian war and the 2014 anti-ISIS mobilization expanded Iranian operational reach from Lebanon into Syria and Iraq, while the 2014 Houthi capture of Sanaa opened a southern pressure point on Saudi Arabia and Red Sea shipping. Tehran's network after 2014 was wider, more militarized, and more embedded in weak states. 1 2 3

  4. 2015-2016

    JCPOA takes effect without changing regional militia strategy The 2015 nuclear agreement and its 16 January 2016 implementation reduced atomic pressure on Tehran, but the IRGC kept backing Hezbollah, Iraqi factions, and other armed partners because the agreement did not settle Iran's contest with Israel or the United States. That split let Tehran separate nuclear bargaining from regional coercion for several years. 1 2

  5. 2023-10-07

    Hamas attack on Israel triggers multi-front war The 7 October 2023 attack pushed Hezbollah, Iraqi factions, and the Houthis into simultaneous action, including Red Sea and border attacks, and ended the prior assumption that fronts could stay compartmentalized. The same campaign exposed how much the network depended on Hezbollah deterrence and Syrian logistics. 1 2 3

  6. 2024-09/2024-12

    Hezbollah attrition and Assad collapse break the land bridge Israeli operations from September 2024 killed senior Hezbollah leaders, forced a November 2024 ceasefire north of the Litani, and the December 2024 fall of Bashar al-Assad severed Tehran's Syrian corridor. Iran then lost the geography that had sustained Hezbollah as its main external deterrent. 1 2 3

  7. 2025-06

    Twelve-Day War between Israel and Iran The June 2025 war broke the taboo against direct Israel-Iran state conflict and proved that Tehran could no longer rely on partners alone to absorb pressure. The war also pushed Gulf governments and Oman toward crisis management rather than alignment with either maximalist camp. 1 2

  8. 2026-02/2026-06

    United States-Israel strikes on Iran and failed June memorandum The February 2026 attack on Iran, which CFR records as killing Supreme Leader Ali Khamenei, turned the contest into open interstate war, and the June 2026 U.S.-Iran memorandum failed to settle Hormuz access, sanctions relief, or the nuclear file. The result by October 2026 is a war that is lower in daily intensity than March 2026 but higher in strategic volatility than any pre-2023 cycle. 1 2 3

Current phase

October 2026 is a phase of fragmented deterrence and punitive raids: Israel is using force to block rearmament across Gaza, Lebanon, Syria, and Iran, while Tehran is using the Houthis, Iraqi factions, and residual Palestinian links to impose shipping, base, and political costs without restoring the pre-2024 Hezbollah-centered shield. 1 2 3 4

What's at stake.

  • Iran: Regime security, its allied network and the oil revenue that funds it.
  • Israel: Freedom to strike perceived threats and the cost of a multi-front war.
  • Gulf states: Their oil, ports and investment plans, which recent attacks have hit directly.
  • China: Discounted Iranian crude and stable Gulf supply.

Counterview. A competing reading says October 2026 is not an Iranian strategic defeat but a harsh transition, because the Houthis still hold Yemen's Red Sea chokepoint, Popular Mobilization Force factions still sit inside Iraq's state budget, and Palestinian anger in Gaza and the West Bank is replenishing recruitment faster than Israel can destroy networks. Under that reading, Benjamin Netanyahu and Donald Trump have removed leaders and supply corridors since 2024 but have not built a political order in Gaza, Lebanon, Syria, or Iraq, so 2027 could produce a looser and harder-to-deter coalition that is less dependent on Tehran's direct command. That case gains strength if Muscat talks fail by January 2027 and if Saudi Arabia or the UAE suffers another major strike before March 2027.
