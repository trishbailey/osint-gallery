India and South Asia

Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history inflection

  1. 1947-1971 Partition, rivalry, and the birth of Bangladesh

    British India was partitioned in August 1947 into India and Pakistan, and Asia for Educators records that nearly 17 million Hindus, Muslims, and Sikhs moved across the new borders. Wars in 1947-48, 1965, and 1971 made interstate security the organizing fact of South Asia, and the December 1971 war produced Bangladesh with Indian military backing.

  2. 1971-1998 Indian predominance, but not regional consent

    New Delhi entered 1972 with unmatched weight after Pakistan lost its eastern wing, yet the region did not become stable. The Government of India sent troops to Sri Lanka in 1987 for a peacekeeping mission that lasted about three years, showing both India’s reach and the political costs of intervention.

  3. 1998-2008 Nuclear deterrence and selective openings

    India tested nuclear devices in May 1998, and Pakistan followed within weeks, turning any future crisis into a nuclear-risk problem. Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee signed the Lahore Declaration in February 1999, but the Kargil war began in May 1999, and the 2005 Srinagar-Muzaffarabad bus service and the 2006 U.S.-India nuclear agreement showed that partial openings could coexist with rivalry.

  4. 2008-2019 Growth divergence and harder political lines

    India’s post-1991 growth path increased the economic gap with most neighbors, while Pakistan entered the 2010s with weaker fiscal space and recurring security strain. Prime Minister Narendra Modi, in office from 2014, paired neighborhood activism with wider diplomacy, and the August 2019 removal of Jammu and Kashmir’s autonomous status led Pakistan to downgrade relations and suspend bilateral trade.

  5. 2020-2024 Regional volatility and more room for outside powers

    Public upheavals in Colombo in July 2022 and in Dhaka in August 2024 showed that domestic legitimacy, not only interstate power, now drives regional alignments. The World Bank reported that social unrest also weighed on Nepal’s 2026 outlook, giving China, Gulf partners, the United States, and international lenders more room to shape outcomes.

  6. 2024-2026 Competitive multi-alignment in the Bay of Bengal

    Post-Hasina Bangladesh moved to diversify external ties after August 2024, while Bangladesh and Pakistan resumed direct trade and Bangladesh imported 50,000 tons of rice from Pakistan in February 2026. In May 2026 India answered by widening ties with the United States, the United Arab Emirates, Europe, and Vietnam, including a U.S.-India Critical Minerals Framework and $5 billion of UAE investment commitments.

Where today sits. Inflection: Late 2026 still leaves India as the region’s main economic center, with the United Nations projecting 6.4-6.6% growth for India in 2026 and the World Bank putting South Asia at 5.8% in 2026 and 6.8% on average in 2026-27. Late 2026 is weaker for New Delhi in Dhaka than early 2024, because Bangladesh diversified ties after August 2024, resumed direct trade with Pakistan, and imported 50,000 tons of rice from Pakistan in February 2026. Trajectory and driving forces

Since 2024, South Asia has been moving toward a looser order in which India keeps economic preponderance but no longer secures automatic alignment from nearby capitals. Dhaka has used the post-August 2024 transition to reopen Pakistan links, deepen China ties, and widen options with the United States, while New Delhi has compensated by pushing external partnerships with Washington, Abu Dhabi, Europe, and Hanoi. The mechanism is straightforward: India offers market size, geography, and faster growth; China offers infrastructure and defense ties; Gulf states offer energy and capital; Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Maldives, and Bhutan use each channel to cut dependence on any single partner. [10] [12] [11] [5] [6] [7] [8] [9] [4]

  • Indian economic asymmetry. The United Nations projected India at 6.6 percent growth in 2026, and that growth gives New Delhi more fiscal room for infrastructure, energy buffers, and diplomatic finance than Pakistan, Sri Lanka, Nepal, or Maldives. This mechanism matters because neighbors can resist Indian preferences politically, but they still need access to India’s market, logistics, or crisis support.
  • China-enabled hedging. China gives Dhaka, Colombo, Kathmandu, and Male an alternative source of infrastructure finance, port activity, and in some cases defense supply, which reduces the cost of saying no to New Delhi. Bangladesh is the most important case in 2026 because its Bay of Bengal location and proximity to India’s Siliguri Corridor let any Chinese or Pakistan link bite more directly into Indian security planning.
  • Frozen India-Pakistan rivalry. The August 2019 Kashmir change and the bilateral trade freeze kept India and Pakistan from rebuilding a routine political channel. That rivalry absorbs military attention in New Delhi and Islamabad and makes smaller states treat every crisis as a chance to widen their diplomatic room.
  • Domestic turnover in neighboring states. Sri Lanka in July 2022, Bangladesh in August 2024, and Nepal’s later unrest showed that leaders who inherit crisis rarely want one external sponsor. New governments or interim authorities often rebalance first for domestic legitimacy, and only later for economic efficiency.
  • Gulf energy and remittance exposure. The United Nations said in 2026 that higher oil prices and weaker remittances from Gulf economies would hit Bangladesh, Pakistan, and Sri Lanka harder than India. This mechanism pushes South Asian governments to court Abu Dhabi, Doha, Riyadh, and Washington even when their main security problem is closer to home.

Turning points

  1. 1947-08

    Partition of British India created India and Pakistan and drove the movement of nearly 17 million people. The 1947 border made territorial disputes and minority protection central to regional politics from the first day. [2] [3]

  2. 1971-12

    The India-Pakistan war of 1971 ended with the creation of Bangladesh. Bangladesh gave India its largest post-colonial strategic gain, but it also created a sovereign neighbor that could later resist Indian preferences. [1] [2]

  3. 1998-05

    India’s May 1998 nuclear tests and Pakistan’s reply turned South Asia into an overtly nuclearized region. After 1998, even limited conventional clashes carried a nuclear escalation problem, which changed every later India-Pakistan crisis. [2]

  4. 1999-02

    Prime Minister Atal Bihari Vajpayee and Prime Minister Nawaz Sharif signed the Lahore Declaration in February 1999. Lahore showed that arms control and travel links were possible even after nuclear tests, setting a recurring pattern of partial opening. [2]

  5. 1999-05

    The Kargil conflict began in May 1999 only months after Lahore. Kargil showed how quickly political openings could be reversed by a localized military gamble in Kashmir. [2]

  6. 2019-08

    India removed Jammu and Kashmir’s autonomous status in August 2019, and Pakistan downgraded relations and suspended bilateral trade. The 2019 break shut the most obvious India-Pakistan reopening path and pushed New Delhi to rely more on deterrence than dialogue. [4] [2]

  7. 2024-08

    Sheikh Hasina’s government fell in August 2024, and Bangladesh entered a transition that reopened its foreign-policy choices. The 2024 change removed India’s most reliable political anchor in Dhaka and gave China and Pakistan a new opening in the Bay of Bengal. [6] [7] [8] [5]

  8. 2026-03

    Chief Adviser Muhammad Yunus traveled to China in March 2026 while Bangladesh-Pakistan trade and military contacts were expanding. March 2026 turned Bangladesh from a bilateral India problem into the central test of whether New Delhi can keep regional primacy without aligned governments next door. [7] [8] [6]

Current phase

Late 2026 is the phase of Indian primacy with weaker deference. India still sets the regional economic pace, but Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, Maldives, and Bhutan now see more value in selective balancing than in tight alignment with New Delhi. [10] [5] [6] [7] [8] [9]

Links to other storylines

  • US-China rivalry and Taiwan: China’s competition with the United States and India gives Bangladesh, Sri Lanka, Nepal, and Maldives more bargaining space, while India uses the Quad, Vietnam, and U.S. critical-minerals ties to offset Chinese reach.
  • Trade wars, tariffs and supply chains: U.S. tariffs and slower global goods trade reduce export room for India and South Asia, which makes domestic demand, bilateral deals, and logistics corridors more valuable.
  • Energy and commodities: Gulf oil prices and Red Sea or Gulf shipping risk hit Pakistan, Sri Lanka, and Bangladesh faster than India, which changes each government’s fiscal room and diplomatic choices.
  • Global governance and alliances: Weak SAARC-style regionalism pushes India toward Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa and partner economies bloc (BRICS), Quad, Gulf, and bilateral channels, while smaller neighbors use the same forum spread to avoid exclusive alignment.

Counterview. India’s neighborhood position may prove more durable than this reading suggests. Bangladesh still lives next to Indian territory and next to the Siliguri Corridor, Nepal and Bhutan remain deeply tied to India’s economy and energy system, and Sri Lanka and Maldives have repeatedly turned back to India when financing or disaster response became urgent. China can finance ports and roads, but Beijing has often been cautious about overt security commitments that would trigger Indian pushback. Pakistan’s economic constraints and Bangladesh’s domestic fragmentation may also cap any coordinated eastern alignment. Under this view, 2024-2026 is a bargaining cycle, not a durable regional realignment.
