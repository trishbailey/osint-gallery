Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history inflection

  1. 2014-2018 China builds a semiconductor substitute program; lithography becomes the first chokepoint.

    Beijing launched the National Integrated Circuit Plan in 2014 with a $26.1 billion state fund, and Washington began urging the Netherlands in 2018 to block ASML EUV sales to Semiconductor Manufacturing International Corporation (SMIC), putting lithography at the center of AI competition.

  2. 2019-2022 Compute scaling turns GPUs into the decisive AI bottleneck.

    By 2021, larger training runs had made compute a primary input to model performance, and Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) wrote in 2022 that more than 90 percent of AI chips used in Chinese data centers were still designed by U.S. companies.

  3. 2022-10 to 2023-10 Washington moves from case-by-case controls to broad China restrictions.

    The U.S. Department of Commerce issued the October 7, 2022 controls on advanced chips, chipmaking tools and U.S. persons' support for Chinese fabs, and the Biden administration expanded the rules in October 2023.

  4. 2024-12 to 2025-01 Controls widen from chips to memory and global diffusion.

    The Department of Commerce added 140 entities and new high-bandwidth memory limits on 2024-12-02, then issued the AI Diffusion Framework in January 2025 to govern shipments beyond China.

  5. 2025-05 to 2025-12 Trump shifts to deal-based export diplomacy and selective reopening.

    The Trump administration said in 2025 it would not enforce the January 2025 diffusion rule, approved large chip exports to the United Arab Emirates (UAE) and Saudi Arabia in May 2025 with security conditions, and later approved some advanced U.S.-origin chip exports to mainland China in December 2025.

  6. 2026 Enforcement focus moves from physical shipment bans to cloud access, location tracking and allied follow-through.

    In 2026, Nvidia told investors it assumes zero China data-center compute revenue and recorded a $4.5 billion H20 charge, while U.S. lawmakers advanced bills on chip location tracking and reporting described Chinese firms renting Nvidia compute in Thailand, Malaysia and Japan.

Where today sits. Inflection: As of 2026-10-25, this storyline sits between the October 7, 2022 shipment-ban regime and a possible 2027 service-access regime. Compared with 2022, Washington in 2026 is debating not only A100-, H100- or Blackwell-class shipments but also high-bandwidth memory, third-country data centers, chip location tracking and remote rental of compute in Thailand, Malaysia and Japan, while Nvidia assumes zero China data-center compute revenue and recorded a $4.5 billion H20 charge. 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8

Trajectory and driving forces

Since 2022, Washington has treated advanced AI chips as dual-use goods and has tried to slow Chinese access at four chokepoints: chip design, chipmaking tools, cloud access and allied enforcement. Over 2025-2026, the Trump administration mixed selective openings for Gulf partners and some China-compliant sales with a refusal to enforce the January 2025 diffusion rule, while Congress moved to codify tighter controls through the Chip Security Act, MATCH Act (United States chip-tool export bill) (MATCH Act) and AI OVERWATCH Act. Carnegie estimated in June 2026 that America's biggest technology companies would spend about $670 billion on AI infrastructure in 2026 and that global spending would approach $1 trillion, so lost China sales did not stop the buildout. Beijing has answered by steering demand toward Huawei, SMIC and domestic memory, and Chinese firms have used overseas clouds where U.S. rules govern hardware more directly than remote access. 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9

  • Compute demand and time-to-power. Carnegie estimated in June 2026 that America's biggest technology companies would spend about $670 billion on AI clusters in 2026, and Carnegie estimated that a one-year delay cuts more than $500 million from the lifetime value of a 100MW U.S. data center; this pushes hyperscalers and sovereign buyers to secure chips, power and permits before they bargain over price.
  • Allied chokepoints in tools, foundries and memory. CSIS and AI Frontiers described the 2022-2026 regime as a chokepoint strategy built on U.S. chip design, Dutch and Japanese toolmakers, Taiwanese foundries and Korean memory, but CSIS also wrote in 2026 that allied legal authorities and enforcement capacity differ, which makes leakage a policy variable rather than a fixed condition.
  • Chinese substitution and state direction. Huawei, SMIC and Chinese state procurement have turned export controls into a demand transfer inside China, while CSIS wrote in 2025 that DeepSeek's rise showed chip controls can slow large-scale deployment more reliably than frontier research.
  • Remote-access leakage through third countries. CNBC reported in 2026 that Moonshot AI, ByteDance, Alibaba and Tencent had been linked by reporting to remote access or attempted access to Nvidia compute in Thailand, Malaysia and Japan, so Congress shifted more attention from product thresholds toward chip location tracking and remote-access legislation.

Turning points

  1. 2014

    China launches the National Integrated Circuit Plan. The $26.1 billion state fund gave Beijing a standing instrument for long-run chip substitution and later let AI demand plug into an existing industrial program. 1

  2. 2018

    Washington leans on the Netherlands over ASML EUV sales to SMIC. The move shifted pressure from finished chips to the lithography bottleneck that China could not easily replace. 1

  3. 2022-10-07

    BIS issues advanced-computing and semiconductor-equipment controls on China. The rules targeted chips, tools and U.S. persons at once and, by CSIS's account, reversed roughly 25 years of U.S. trade policy toward China. 1 2 3

  4. 2023-10

    The Biden administration expands the 2022 rules. The October 2023 update reduced the value of Nvidia's first China-specific redesigns and showed that product downgrades would face repeated review. 1 2

  5. 2024-12-02

    The Department of Commerce adds 140 entities and tightens high-bandwidth memory (HBM) and Foreign Direct Product Rule (FDPR) controls. The 2024 package widened controls from GPUs and tools to memory and third-country production links, making the regime more full-stack. 1

  6. 2025-01

    The Department of Commerce issues the AI Diffusion Framework. The January 2025 rule tried to move from a China-only regime to a global tiered licensing system for advanced compute. 1 2

  7. 2025-12

    The Trump administration approves some advanced U.S.-origin chip exports to mainland China. The December 2025 decision showed that White House policy would balance market share and control rather than hold to blanket denial. 1

  8. 2026-07

    Remote access to overseas graphics processing unit (GPU) clusters becomes a policy target. Reporting on Moonshot AI's alleged use of GB300 compute in Thailand and congressional backing for the Chip Security Act and related bills moved the debate from shipping chips to governing who can use them and where. 1 2 3

Current phase

The October 2026 phase is a bifurcated compute market: U.S. and allied buyers absorb most frontier GPU supply in domestic and partner data centers, while Washington shifts from banning shipments to policing access, location and end use. 1 2 3 4
