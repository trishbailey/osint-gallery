Climate, food and water security

Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history inflection

  1. 1961-1990 Intensification of food production and irrigation

    United Nations Food and Agriculture Organization (FAO) and IPCC data show that global food supply per capita rose by more than 30 percent after 1961, while nitrogen fertiliser use rose by about 800 percent and irrigation water use rose by more than 100 percent. This 1961-1990 phase cut hunger in many regions but embedded a food model that depended on heavy withdrawals from rivers, lakes and aquifers.

  2. 1991-2006 Water stress becomes structural

    FAO and Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) reporting describes agriculture as the dominant water user at about 72 percent of global freshwater withdrawals, so population growth and urban demand tightened competition in basins from South Asia to the Middle East. By the 2000s, water management had become a production constraint rather than a local utility issue.

  3. 2007-2010 Food-market transmission is exposed

    The 2007-2008 food price crisis added 75 million people to chronic hunger, and CSIS records that world wheat prices rose 72 percent. CSIS also records that recurrent Australian droughts accounted for more than one fifth of that wheat-price shock, showing how a climate shock in one exporter could move through trade into global food access.

  4. 2011-2019 Climate enters security and food planning

    The 2010 United States National Security Strategy named climate change as a security issue, and the 2014 United States defence review continued that shift inside security institutions. The IPCC later judged that climate change was already affecting availability, access, utilisation and stability across food systems, with undernutrition worsening after 2014 in parts of Sub-Saharan Africa, South-Eastern Asia and Western Asia.

  5. 2020-2025 Chronic water stress meets repeated food emergencies

    The United Nations says 2.4 billion people now live in water-stressed countries, and FAO says freshwater resources per person have fallen 20 percent over the past two decades. The 2026 Global Report on Food Crises counted 266 million people in acute food insecurity across 47 countries and territories in 2025, with conflict first and weather extremes second among the main drivers.

  6. 2026-2027 Climate shock risk rises as aid capacity falls

    Famine Early Warning Systems Network (FEWS NET) says El Niño conditions emerged in June 2026, reached very strong levels of at least 2 degrees Celsius in September 2026, and are assessed by FEWS NET as very highly certain to persisting through the Northern Hemisphere fall and winter of 2026-2027. The European Commission Joint Research Centre says net official development assistance is projected to fall 6.9 percent to United States dollars (USD) 152 billion in 2026, while humanitarian ODA is projected to fall 40.3 percent and food aid 44.5 percent between 2024 and 2026.

Where today sits. Inflection: Late 2026 sits above the 2010s chronic-exposure baseline, with FAO reporting 2.4 billion people in water-stressed countries and the 2026 Global Report on Food Crises counting 266 million people in acute food insecurity across 47 countries and territories in 2025. Late 2026 is still below the 2007-2008 global supply breakdown, because FAO reported relatively comfortable cereal stocks after the 2025 harvest, but FEWS NET said El Niño reached at least 2 degrees Celsius in September 2026 while the European Commission Joint Research Centre projected humanitarian ODA down 40.3% and food aid down 44.5% between 2024 and 2026. Trajectory and driving forces

Since the 2023 United Nations Water Conference and the 2025-2026 food-crisis assessments, United Nations, FAO, World Food Programme (WFP) and FEWS NET reporting has shifted from treating drought as a seasonal hazard to treating climate-food-water stress as a standing systems problem. The mechanism in late 2026 is cumulative: decades of groundwater and river overuse leave lower starting storage, a very strong El Niño can shift rainfall across several producing and importing regions at once, and a 2024-2026 drop in humanitarian finance weakens the buffer that previously kept local crop failures from becoming wider nutrition and migration crises. The gainers in this phase are producers with secure irrigation, storage and fiscal space, while the losers are import-dependent, conflict-affected and aid-dependent states in Africa, the Middle East and South Asia. [4] [9] [5] [6] [10] [8]

  • Water overdraw and allocation pressure. FAO says agriculture takes 72 percent of freshwater withdrawals, and FAO says freshwater resources per person fell 20 percent in the past two decades. Each drought therefore starts from lower river, reservoir and aquifer levels, and cities, power systems and farms bid against each other more quickly than in the 1990s.
  • Heat and rainfall volatility. FEWS NET says the 2026 El Niño strengthened rapidly to very strong levels by September 2026, and the FAO-WMO findings cited by the Council on Strategic Risks link each 1 degree Celsius of warming to yield losses of 6 percent for wheat, 1.2 percent for rice, 7.5 percent for maize and 6.8 percent for soybeans. Wheat, rice, maize and soy systems in 2026-2027 therefore face lower average yields and larger year-to-year swings.
  • Fragility, conflict and weak governance. UNCCD says drought acts as a threat multiplier rather than a stand-alone cause of violence, and WFP says conflict remained the primary driver of acute hunger in 2025. In Sudan, South Sudan, Somalia, Syria and Sahelian states, damaged markets and institutions convert rainfall shocks into hunger faster than in fiscally stronger states.
  • Trade, energy and infrastructure coupling. UNCCD says low water availability can constrain hydropower, inland shipping and cooling for thermal and nuclear plants, while FAO market reporting says energy prices, fertilizer costs and shipping routes now shape food prices alongside harvests. A drought in one basin can therefore raise power costs, cut pumping and storage, and lift import bills in another region.
  • Shrinking external buffers. The European Commission Joint Research Centre says net ODA is projected at USD 152 billion in 2026, with humanitarian ODA down 40.3 percent and food aid down 44.5 percent from 2024 levels. The second-order effect is that early warning, nutrition programmes and cash transfers reach fewer households just as El Niño risk rises into 2027.

Turning points

  1. 1961

    The post-1961 expansion of irrigation and fertiliser use lifted global food supply per person. IPCC data show that the same production model raised irrigation use by more than 100 percent and fertiliser use by about 800 percent, which created later water and energy exposure. [1]

  2. 2007-2008

    The global food price crisis transmitted exporter drought into global hunger. CSIS records a 72 percent jump in world wheat prices and 75 million additional people in chronic hunger, which demonstrated how weather, energy prices and trade policy could reinforce each other. [2]

  3. 2010

    The United States National Security Strategy treated climate change as a security issue. The 2010 document moved food and water stress from development ministries into defence and foreign-policy planning. [3]

  4. 2019

    The IPCC Special Report on Climate Change and Land set out the four-pillar food-security framework under climate stress. The IPCC conclusion gave governments a common basis for treating availability, access, utilisation and stability as one system rather than separate sector problems. [1]

  5. 2023-03

    United Nations member states elevated water governance at the 2023 Water Conference. FAO linked water scarcity, agriculture and food security more explicitly after the conference because agriculture accounts for 72 percent of freshwater withdrawals. [4]

  6. 2025

    Acute food insecurity reached 266 million people across 47 countries and territories. The 2025 figure showed that conflict, weather extremes and economic shocks were compounding rather than alternating. [5] [11] [8]

  7. 2026-08

    The European Commission Joint Research Centre warned that El Niño risk was rising while aid budgets were falling. The Joint Research Centre projected a 40.3 percent fall in humanitarian ODA and a 44.5 percent fall in food aid between 2024 and 2026, reducing response capacity before the 2026-2027 climate shock window. [8]

  8. 2026-09

    FEWS NET classified the 2026 El Niño as very strong. FEWS NET placed the event at at least 2 degrees Celsius in September 2026 and assessed as very highly certain to last through the 2026-2027 boreal winter, which raises the odds of synchronized crop, pasture and water stress across several regions. [6]

Current phase

Late 2026 is a pre-impact consolidation phase: global cereal availability is still buffered by the 2025 harvest, but local access is deteriorating where conflict, import dependence and water depletion are already high. [6] [11] [7] [8]

Historical analogue: 1997-1998 El Niño with positive Indian Ocean Dipole, 1997-1998

Why comparable. FEWS NET says the 2026 event resembles 1997-1998 in rapid intensification and the projected October-December positive Indian Ocean Dipole, which can stack drought and flood risks across East Africa, Southern Africa, South Asia and Latin America.

Where it breaks. UNU-INWEH and FAO descriptions point to a lower starting point in 2026 because decades of over-withdrawal have depleted aquifers and freshwater systems, and the European Commission Joint Research Centre describes much weaker humanitarian financing than in earlier cycles.

What happened then. The 1997-1998 event produced multi-region harvest and infrastructure shocks during 1998, and FEWS NET lists it as one of the strongest El Niño episodes since 1950. [12] [6] [8]

Links to other storylines

  • Migration and displacement: FEWS NET, WFP and UNCCD evidence indicates that drought, food-price stress and failing rural livelihoods raise movement from rural districts to cities and then across borders, especially in the Horn of Africa, the Sahel and Syria.
  • Trade wars, tariffs and supply chains: FAO and CSIS material shows that drought in exporters, shipping disruptions and fertilizer costs can move through grain markets quickly, so climate stress is also a trade and price story.
  • Energy and commodities: UNCCD notes that low water availability can cut hydropower, inland shipping and plant cooling, while FAO market analysis links gas and fuel prices to fertilizer and food costs.
  • Global governance and alliances: The 2024-2026 fall in ODA and food aid described by the European Commission Joint Research Centre turns climate-food-water pressure into a test of multilateral finance, early warning and burden-sharing.

Counterview. The strongest case against a darker trajectory is that FAO market reporting still shows near-record global cereal availability after the 2025 harvest, FEWS NET says Southern Africa entered the 2026-2027 season with a cereal harvest nearly 10 percent above the five-year average, and WFP says conflict rather than weather is still the first driver of acute hunger in 2025. In that reading, 2026-2027 brings severe regional emergencies but not a new global climate-food regime, because storage, trade, irrigation, insurance and anticipatory action absorb the shock in most markets.
