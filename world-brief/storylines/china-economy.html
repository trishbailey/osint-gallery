China's economy and global effects

Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history inflection

  1. 1949-1978 Central planning and early industrialization

    Mao Zedong's PRC built a state-led industrial base after 1949, but the Great Leap Forward of 1958-1962 and the Cultural Revolution of 1966-1976 imposed large human and economic costs before market reform began.

  2. 1978-2001 Reform and opening

    Deng Xiaoping's 1978 reform program shifted China toward markets, special economic zones and foreign trade, and World Bank data say gross domestic product (GDP) growth averaged over 9 percent a year after 1978 while almost 800 million people exited extreme poverty.

  3. 2001-2008 World Trade Organization (WTO) export surge

    China's 2001 WTO entry locked the PRC into global manufacturing chains and pushed growth toward exports, investment and urban construction.

  4. 2008-2015 Credit and property supercycle

    Beijing's 2008-2009 response to the global financial crisis used credit, infrastructure and housing to protect employment, but the 2010s model also raised local-government and developer leverage.

  5. 2015-2020 Slower growth and industrial upgrading

    Xi Jinping's leadership accepted slower expansion, with growth slowing from 14.2 percent in 2007 to 6.6 percent in 2018, while programs such as Made in China 2025 pushed advanced manufacturing and technology.

  6. 2020-2026 Property unwind and export cushioning

    Beijing's 2020 debt curbs on developers cut financing, real estate and construction fell from 16 percent of nominal GDP growth in 2017-2019 to almost zero after 2021, and 2025-2026 growth near 4.5 percent has depended on exports, targeted fiscal support and new industrial sectors more than on housing or broad household demand.

Where today sits. Inflection: October 2026 sits far below the old growth regime, with the National Bureau of Statistics reporting 4.5% GDP growth in the third quarter of 2026 versus 14.2% in 2007 and more than 9% average growth after 1978. October 2026 also sits near a high point in external manufacturing reach, with a $133.5 billion monthly trade surplus in September 2026, and near a low point in the property model, with real-estate investment down 20.6% in January-September 2026 and real estate and construction contributing almost nothing to nominal growth after 2021. Trajectory and driving forces

Since late 2024, Li Qiang's State Council and the People's Bank of China have used targeted fiscal expansion, special bonds and selective easing to keep growth near the 4.5-5 percent official range, while Xi Jinping's leadership has refused a return to property-led stimulus. Since 2025, Chinese exporters have offset weak household demand through market diversification, lower prices and stronger sales of technology goods, which has preserved employment and tax revenue but widened trade friction with the United States, the European Union and other manufacturers. Gerard DiPippo's September 2025 review noted that China reported 5.2 percent real GDP growth in the second quarter of 2025 but only 3.9 percent nominal growth, so wages, revenues and budgets felt far weaker than the headline. In 2026, coastal exporters and state-backed manufacturers gain more than private service firms, developers and land-dependent local governments, which is why household confidence and local fiscal capacity have not recovered as quickly as headline GDP. By late 2026, Beijing's declared aim has shifted from short-term housing rescue to a slower rebalancing toward household demand, social support, a more unified domestic market and advanced manufacturing, but the handover is incomplete because local governments, developers and households are still repairing balance sheets. [1] [9] [10] [11] [2] [12] [13] [14] [15] [8]

  • Property and local-government balance sheets. After Beijing's 2020 debt curbs on developers and the 2021-2026 housing downturn, Chinese local governments lost land-sale income, banks faced weaker asset quality and households saw housing wealth fall; by January-September 2026 real-estate investment was still down 20.6 percent, so every new yuan of fiscal support faced debt repair before fresh spending.
  • Export competitiveness and market diversification. In 2025-2026, Chinese manufacturers defended volumes through lower prices, full supplier networks and sales beyond the United States; Bruegel counted 6.1 percent export growth in the first seven months of 2025 even after US tariffs rose as high as 145 percent before May 2025 relief to 30 percent.
  • State-directed industrial upgrading. Xi Jinping's program for advanced manufacturing directs credit, procurement and research toward EVs, batteries, solar equipment, artificial intelligence (AI) hardware and other new-economy sectors; UBS estimated these sectors at 15-20 percent of GDP in 2024 and about one quarter of growth in 2020-2024.
  • Household caution and weak price formation. AMRO said private consumption contributed more than half of GDP growth in the first half of 2025, yet income growth, property wealth losses and a thin welfare system kept household saving high and left CPI at 0.0 percent in 2025 and only 0.4 percent in 2026.
  • External pressure and Beijing's policy choice. Donald Trump's 2025 tariff escalation and wider controls on technology and investment strengthened Beijing's case for lower reliance on foreign inputs, so Chinese leaders now treat slower short-term growth as the price of lower strategic dependence.

Turning points

  1. 1978-12

    Deng Xiaoping launches reform and opening. The CCP moved from central planning toward market incentives, special economic zones and trade, which set up four decades of export- and investment-led growth. [3] [4] [5]

  2. 2001-12-11

    China joins the World Trade Organization. WTO entry embedded China in global manufacturing and accelerated the export, urbanization and construction model that dominated the 2000s. [4] [16]

  3. 2008-11

    Beijing answers the global financial crisis with credit and infrastructure expansion. The State Council used investment to replace lost foreign demand, but the mechanism tied local finance more tightly to land sales and property development through the 2010s. [4] [5]

  4. 2015-05

    Made in China 2025 and related industrial policies move upgrading to the center. Xi Jinping's leadership sought higher-value manufacturing as growth slowed from the double digits of the 2000s and as leaders judged the old debt-and-property model less durable. [4] [17]

  5. 2020-08

    Beijing imposes debt curbs on developers. The financing squeeze after 2020 ended the long housing expansion and turned real estate from a growth engine into a drag on households, banks and local governments. [1]

  6. 2024-11

    China shifts to a more proactive fiscal stance after September 2024 People's Bank of China (PBOC) guidance. Beijing accepted that domestic demand and local-government balance sheets required more central support, but leaders still chose targeted measures over a nationwide housing reflation. [10] [11]

  7. 2025-05

    Washington cuts peak 2025 tariffs from 145 percent to 30 percent after direct US-China trade was hit. The tariff shock pushed Chinese firms harder toward third markets, lower pricing and technology exports, which kept headline growth up but widened foreign concern about excess industrial supply. [11]

  8. 2026

    Beijing elevates household demand in planning for the 15th Five-Year Plan period. Asia Society's reading of the Fourth Plenum and CEWC agenda says the leadership now treats consumption as a top task because export support will not by itself repair employment, prices or local finance. [9] [12] [13]

Current phase

China in October 2026 is in a managed transition: Beijing is keeping GDP near target with exports, infrastructure and selective fiscal support while letting the property share of the economy shrink and trying to widen household demand without a large housing bailout. The current arrangement buys Xi Jinping time in 2026, but it also extends pressure on local governments, private employers and household income growth. [9] [12] [13] [8]

Historical analogue: Japan post-bubble adjustment, 1991-2002

Why comparable. Japan after 1991 combined a property bust, weak household demand, bank strain and export strength, which is close to China's 2020-2026 mix of housing stress and manufacturing resilience.

Where it breaks. China in 2026 has capital controls, state-owned banks, a large external surplus and a lower income level, so Beijing has more administrative control than Tokyo had in 1991.

What happened then. Japan's 1991 property bust was followed by low nominal growth through the 1990s and policy rates near zero by 1999. [18] [11] [19]

Links to other storylines

  • Trade wars, tariffs and supply chains: China's export volumes, large trade surplus and low producer prices keep tariffs, anti-dumping cases and supply-chain diversification high on the trade agenda for Washington, Brussels, New Delhi and Brasília.
  • AI race, compute and chip export controls: Xi Jinping's reliance on advanced manufacturing, AI hardware and semiconductors makes US, Dutch, Japanese and Taiwanese chip controls a direct input into China's 2027 growth mix.
  • Energy and commodities: China's move from apartments and steel toward EVs, batteries, solar equipment and power grids shifts global demand from iron ore and coking coal toward copper, lithium, nickel, electricity equipment and rare earths.
  • Central banks, inflation and debt: Chinese factory prices and export prices affect inflation baskets in the euro area, the United States and much of Asia, while any larger Beijing stimulus would push up oil and metals prices that the Federal Reserve and the European Central Bank (ECB) track.

Counterview. A stronger reading starts from three 2025-2026 facts: AMRO said private consumption contributed more than half of GDP growth in the first half of 2025, UBS estimated new-economy sectors at 15-20 percent of GDP in 2024, and China's September 2026 trade surplus hit $133.5 billion. On that reading, Xi Jinping has already replaced much of the old housing engine with services, advanced manufacturing and central fiscal support, so 4.3-4.5 percent growth in 2027 would represent durability rather than drift. On that reading, a large household-transfer push in 2027 could misallocate scarce fiscal capacity away from sectors that still win export market share.
