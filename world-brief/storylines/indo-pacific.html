Indo-Pacific flashpoints

Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history high point

  1. 1951-1972 U.S. alliance architecture and the Korean War legacy

    The United States fixed the first security map with the 1951 Mutual Defense Treaty with the Philippines, the 1953 Korean Armistice, and the post-1945 alliance system in Japan and South Korea. That 1951-1953 structure made the Korean Peninsula, the Ryukyu chain and the Philippine archipelago the main U.S. forward positions in maritime Asia.

  2. 1972-1995 Rapprochement with China, but growing maritime claim activity

    The 1972 U.S.-China opening and the 1979 normalization reduced direct U.S.-China military confrontation, but the 1982 UNCLOS framework gave new salience to maritime jurisdiction and exclusive economic zones. China’s 1995 move onto Mischief Reef showed that disputes in the South China Sea were moving from diplomatic maps to permanent facts on the water.

  3. 1995-2012 Taiwan deterrence and East-South China Sea linkage

    The 1995-1996 Taiwan Strait crisis and repeated frictions around the East and South China Seas tied Taiwan security to wider first-island-chain planning. The 2012 Scarborough Shoal standoff and the 2012 Senkaku confrontation then pushed Beijing, Manila and Tokyo into a more durable contest over day-to-day administrative control.

  4. 2013-2018 Artificial-island construction and legal response

    From 2013 to 2016, China built and fortified artificial-island positions in the Spratlys, changing local reach for coast guard, naval and air operations. The July 2016 arbitral ruling in the Philippines case rejected China’s claim to historic rights within the nine-dash line, and the 2018 U.S. decision to disinvite China from RIMPAC showed that Washington was beginning to answer with military and diplomatic costs.

  5. 2019-2024 Routine pressure and alliance networking

    From 2019 to 2024, China Coast Guard patrols around the Senkakus became near-continuous and pressure on Philippine and Taiwanese positions became more regular. From 2023, the United States, Japan and the Philippines moved from parallel bilateral ties toward a trilateral framework, while President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. reoriented Philippine planning toward archipelagic and maritime defense.

  6. 2025-2026 Simultaneous flashpoints and operational trilateralism

    Beijing’s September 2025 designation of Scarborough Shoal as a nature reserve added an administrative layer to the South China Sea contest. Armed Conflict Location and Event Data project (ACLED) recorded about a 490% increase in Chinese force movements in the Philippine Exclusive Economic Zone from January to June 2026 versus the previous six months, while the United States and Japan accelerated force posture and exercise activity linked to both the South China Sea and Taiwan.

Where today sits. High point: October 2026 sits above the post-2012 baseline in operational tempo, with ACLED recording about a 490% rise in Chinese force movements in the Philippine Exclusive Economic Zone in January-June 2026 versus the previous six months and Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) describing near-continuous China Coast Guard pressure around the Senkakus. October 2026 still sits below declared blockade, seizure of a major Philippine outpost, or direct U.S.-China combat, but United States, Japan and Philippines coordination is deeper than during 2019-2024. Trajectory and driving forces

From 2023 to 2026, Indo-Pacific flashpoints shifted from mostly separate bilateral disputes toward a linked contest across the first island chain, with the Philippines, Japan, Taiwan and the Korean Peninsula increasingly appearing in the same operational planning. Beijing has favored coast guard, maritime militia, survey-vessel and administrative measures because those tools can widen day-to-day control near Scarborough Shoal, Sabina Shoal, the Senkakus and waters near Taiwan without the costs of declared war. Washington, Tokyo and Manila have answered with access, maritime domain awareness, joint drills and contingency planning because basing in Okinawa, Luzon and nearby waters matters in both a South China Sea crisis and a Taiwan contingency. The next 12 to 24 months will likely turn on whether China can make repeated pressure activity look routine faster than U.S.-allied coordination can harden into standing operational habits. [8] [1] [3] [9] [4] [5] [10] [7] [11] [12]

  • Chinese sub-war pressure. Beijing uses coast guard, militia, survey ships and legal-administrative measures because those tools let China press maritime claims in 2026 without triggering the political and economic costs of a formal war with the United States, Japan or the Philippines.
  • U.S.-Japan-Philippines trilateralization. Washington, Tokyo and Manila are building a practical triad because China’s activity links the East China Sea, the South China Sea and Taiwan in one military geography, and because no U.S. plan in those waters works well without access from Japan and the Philippines.
  • Japanese and Philippine defense reorientation. Japan’s move toward 2% of gross domestic product (GDP) for defense by 2030 and the Philippines’ Comprehensive Archipelagic Defence Concept both push resources toward maritime surveillance, access, logistics and anti-ship reach, which directly affects the East China Sea, Luzon Strait and South China Sea.
  • Taiwan contingency linkage. Any Taiwan blockade or major Taiwan crisis would pull in Ryukyu, Luzon and Batanes geography, so actions near Philippine shoals and around Japan now carry added weight because the same runways, ports and sensor networks matter in both theaters.
  • Korean Peninsula spillover risk. North Korea’s ties with China and Russia give Beijing and Washington one more live theater to factor into force allocation, which means events in Dandong, the Yellow Sea or the Korean Peninsula can tighten choices for U.S. allies in Japan and the Philippines.

Turning points

  1. 1951

    The United States and the Philippines signed the Mutual Defense Treaty in 1951. The 1951 treaty turned Philippine territory and nearby waters into part of the U.S. alliance system, which still shapes every South China Sea contingency discussion in 2026. [2]

  2. 1995

    China occupied Mischief Reef in 1995. The 1995 move showed that maritime disputes in the South China Sea could produce permanent physical change, not just legal disagreement, and it pushed Manila to reassess the costs of weak sea denial and weak presence. [4]

  3. 2012

    China established de facto control at Scarborough Shoal in 2012, while tensions around the Senkakus also hardened in 2012. The 2012 episodes linked East China Sea and South China Sea contests through the same method: frequent law-enforcement and presence operations designed to test who can sustain administrative control without firing first. [1] [13] [3]

  4. 2013-2016

    China built and militarized artificial-island positions in the Spratly Islands from 2013 to 2016. The 2013-2016 construction program extended Chinese air, naval and surveillance reach and made future pressure on Philippine, Vietnamese and U.S. forces more sustainable from fixed positions. [8] [1] [6]

  5. 2016-07

    An arbitral tribunal ruled in July 2016 for the Philippines on core South China Sea issues. The July 2016 ruling gave Manila a durable legal basis under UNCLOS, but it did not by itself change control at sea, which widened the gap between legal position and operational position. [13] [2]

  6. 2023-03

    Japan, the Philippines and the United States moved toward a trilateral national-security framework in March 2023, and Manila reoriented defense planning under President Ferdinand Marcos Jr. The 2023 shift mattered because Washington, Tokyo and Manila stopped treating East China Sea, South China Sea and Taiwan problems as separate files and began building a shared planning habit. [4] [5]

  7. 2025-09

    Beijing designated Scarborough Shoal a national nature reserve in September 2025. The September 2025 step added a domestic administrative layer to Chinese maritime presence, which is a standard way to turn temporary patrol patterns into an argument for lasting jurisdiction. [3]

  8. 2026-01/2026-06

    ACLED recorded about a 490% increase in Chinese force movements in the Philippine Exclusive Economic Zone from January to June 2026 versus the previous six months. The January-June 2026 surge showed that the contest had moved from episodic confrontation to a much denser operational tempo, which raises the chance of collision, exhaustion and command error. [3]

Current phase

The current phase is cumulative pressure versus distributed deterrence, with Beijing using non-war maritime and administrative tools and Washington, Tokyo and Manila building a semi-operational triad across Japan and the Philippines. [1] [3] [4] [5]

Historical analogue: Senkaku coast guard endurance contest, 2012-2026

Why comparable. The East China Sea contest around the Senkakus resembles the South China Sea pattern because China and Japan have tested administrative control through coast guard presence, long loiter times and constant readiness strain rather than through open naval battle.

Where it breaks. The Senkaku case differs because Japan has stronger coast guard capacity, no exposed rusting outpost like Sierra Madre, and a more consolidated alliance structure with the United States than the Philippines has at remote Spratly features.

What happened then. CSIS described year-over-year records in China Coast Guard time spent around the Senkakus, which shows how a contest that began in 2012 can persist for more than a decade without formal war. [1] [13]

Links to other storylines

  • US-China rivalry and Taiwan: Any Taiwan blockade or major Taiwan crisis would pull in Okinawa, the Luzon Strait and Philippine access points, so pressure near Philippine features and Japanese waters directly affects the Taiwan military balance.
  • Trade wars, tariffs and supply chains: The South China Sea and waters near Taiwan carry shipping, insurance and manufacturing exposure for Japan, South Korea, Southeast Asia and the United States, so every maritime spike can feed freight costs and stockpiling decisions.
  • Energy and commodities: Japan’s energy dependence on sea lanes through the South China Sea means pressure in those waters affects Tokyo’s defense policy, tanker routing and reserve planning.
  • AI race, compute and chip export controls: Taiwan’s chip production makes military pressure around the island a problem for data-center buildouts, defense electronics and export-control policy far beyond East Asia.

Counterview. The strongest counterview says 2026 looks worse in incident data than in actual war risk. Xi Jinping’s government still faces strong incentives to protect coastal trade and avoid a fight with the United States, Tokyo and Manila still lack the capacity to reverse Chinese presence at distant features, and Washington still has reasons to keep treaty language flexible enough to deter allied adventurism as well as Chinese force. On that reading, 2027 brings more patrols, more exercises and more domestic legal steps, but not the blockade rehearsal or lethal collision that would force a strategic choice.

    Why: Manila wants repeated combined presence inside its exclusive economic zone to thicken deterrence without invoking treaty-crisis language. Tokyo wants operational habits with Manila and Washington that fit southwest islands defense planning. Washington wants a semi-routine triad that raises the cost of Chinese coercion below the level of open conflict. The Philippines gains more usable procedures for resupply, emergency response and maritime monitoring. Japan and the United States gain a more credible distributed posture. Beijing loses room to describe trilateral activity as occasional symbolism and may answer with more coast guard or militia pressure at contested shoals.
