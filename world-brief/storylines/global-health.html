Global health and biosecurity

Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history inflection

  1. 1948-1968 Founding the post-war health order

    The World Health Organization entered into force on 7 April 1948, built a global disease-tracking service, and used mass campaigns against tuberculosis, malaria, yaws, syphilis, smallpox and leprosy to turn ad hoc sanitary cooperation into a standing system.

  2. 1969-2002 Rules, immunization and eradication

    The World Health Assembly established the first International Health Regulations in 1969, and the Expanded Programme on Immunization from 1974 helped drive smallpox eradication in 1980, but compliance still depended on national laboratories, clinics and border authorities.

  3. 2003-2013 severe acute respiratory syndrome (SARS) and the modern health-security turn

    The 2003 SARS outbreak caused an estimated $30 billion in losses and pushed governments toward the 2005 International Health Regulations revision, which widened notification and response duties for a jet-age outbreak environment.

  4. 2014-2019 Ebola and capacity-building by evaluation

    The Global Health Security Agenda launched on 13 February 2014 with 44 nations, and the West Africa Ebola epidemic then produced more than 22,000 cases and nearly 9,000 deaths, which pushed outbreak readiness into national-security planning and later into country evaluation processes used in more than 100 states.

  5. 2020-2024 coronavirus disease 2019 (COVID-19) shock and technology acceleration

    COVID-19 caused more than 7 million confirmed deaths, as many as 20 million excess deaths, and $13.8 trillion in global gross domestic product (GDP) losses by 2024, while mRNA platforms, genomic sequencing and digital surveillance expanded much faster than the legal and financing system around them.

  6. 2025-2027 Reform without fiscal depth

    The 2025 World Health Organization (WHO) Pandemic Agreement and the 2024 International Health Regulations (IHR) amendments opened a new rules phase, but 2026 brought contraction, with WHO planning a 25% headcount decline by June 2026, Gavi facing a 24% replenishment drop, the Global Fund a 37% drop, and WHO members still needing a Pathogen Access and Benefit-Sharing system (PABS) deal by May 2027 before the broader agreement can be ratified and enter into force.

Where today sits. Inflection: October 2026 combines more legal text than the 2014 Ebola period, through the 2025 WHO Pandemic Agreement and the 2024 IHR amendments, with less fiscal depth than governments promised after 2020. WHO planned a 25% headcount decline by June 2026, Gavi faced a 24% replenishment drop, the Global Fund faced a 37% drop, and an Ebola emergency in central Africa is testing the system before the PABS deadline in May 2027. Trajectory and driving forces

Since 2024, the direction has been from post-coronavirus disease (COVID) institution-building toward a thinner, more regional and more security-framed system. WHO members approved new rules, but donor cuts in 2025-2026 forced WHO, Gavi, the Global Fund and United Nations Children's Fund (UNICEF) into workforce reductions and restructuring at the same time that the Global Preparedness Monitoring Board reported more frequent and more damaging outbreaks. The result in late 2026 is not the end of global health cooperation; it is a shift from broad multilateral expansion toward selective financing for surveillance, countermeasures and national resilience, with lower-income ministries carrying more execution risk. [9] [13] [10] [14] [11] [15] [16] [17] [8]

  • Donor retrenchment and fiscal competition. WHO, UNICEF, Gavi and the Global Fund lost money or staff in 2025-2026 because donor governments redirected budgets toward debt service, domestic politics and defence, so surveillance, immunization and emergency-response functions now compete harder for each dollar; finance ministries gain discretion, while low-income health ministries lose buffer capacity.
  • Incomplete rules on pathogen sharing and benefits. WHO members adopted a Pandemic Agreement in 2025, but the PABS annex still needs agreement by May 2027, so sample sharing, sequence sharing and benefit sharing for vaccines and drugs still rest on bargaining rather than automatic rules; pharmaceutical firms want clearer access terms, while many lower-income states want firmer return guarantees.
  • A wider threat set using the same weak systems. In 2026, the Global Preparedness Monitoring Board and IFPMA treated zoonotic spillover, measles and polio backsliding, antimicrobial resistance, and deliberate or accidental laboratory events as one preparedness problem; IFPMA cited a 2025-2050 antimicrobial-resistance death estimate of 39 million to argue that slow-burn threats can outrun acute outbreaks.
  • Technology cuts both ways. By 2026, artificial intelligence (AI), sequencing, CRISPR diagnostics and mRNA platforms could shorten discovery and response times, but open biological design tools also lowered technical barriers for misuse and made pathogen-agnostic early warning more urgent; countries that can pair data systems with surge manufacturing gain leverage over those that cannot.

Turning points

  1. 1948-04-07

    WHO Constitution enters into force WHO gave member states a permanent Geneva institution, a constitutional mandate and a disease-tracking function, which turned episodic sanitary conferences into standing global coordination. [1]

  2. 1969

    World Health Assembly establishes the first International Health Regulations The World Health Assembly gave cross-border outbreak reporting a formal legal base, but enforcement still depended on national laboratories and border authorities. [1]

  3. 2003

    SARS outbreak drives the 2005 IHR revision SARS showed that jet-age spread and a $30 billion economic hit could outrun 20th-century reporting rules, which pushed governments toward wider notification and response duties in 2005. [4] [3] [18]

  4. 2014-02-13

    Global Health Security Agenda launches as Ebola emerges in West Africa President Obama and 43 partner governments moved outbreak readiness into leader-level security planning just as Ebola exposed how weak local surveillance could become an international crisis within months. [5] [6] [7]

  5. 2020-03-11

    WHO declares COVID-19 a pandemic COVID-19 exposed gaps in supply chains, data sharing and countermeasure access on a scale that produced more than 7 million confirmed deaths and $13.8 trillion in GDP losses by 2024. [4] [8]

  6. 2024-06

    WHO members adopt IHR amendments WHO members tried to tighten notification, sequencing and response rules after COVID-19, but the legal upgrade still depended on financing and political compliance in capitals. [12]

  7. 2025-05

    WHO Pandemic Agreement is adopted with PABS still unresolved Member states created a new framework for prevention, preparedness and response, but the unresolved PABS annex left sample and sequence access tied to further negotiation through May 2027. [10] [9] [8]

  8. 2026-06

    Post-COVID funding contraction becomes operational WHO, Gavi, the Global Fund and UNICEF entered 2026 with restructuring and workforce cuts, which turned 2026 into the first field test of whether post-COVID rules can work with less money and fewer staff. [9] [11]

Current phase

The current phase in late 2026 is an austerity test of the post-COVID architecture: WHO members have updated rules and faster technologies, but the institutions meant to run surveillance, immunization and surge response are being downsized while live outbreaks continue. [10] [9] [11] [13] [8]

Links to other storylines

  • Global governance and alliances: WHO financing, the PABS annex and the 2026-2027 PPPR follow-up process make biosecurity a test of whether multilateral rules can still deliver public goods after donor retrenchment.
  • Climate, food and water security: Climate pressure, land-use change and animal-human contact raise zoonotic spillover risk, so veterinary surveillance and food-system resilience in 2026 sit closer to outbreak prevention than they did in the 2014 Ebola cycle.
  • AI race, compute and chip export controls: By 2026, AI models, sequencing compute and export controls affect who can design diagnostics, screen antibiotics and run pathogen-agnostic surveillance at scale, so compute access is becoming part of health security.
  • Trade wars, tariffs and supply chains: Vaccines, PPE, reagents and genomic equipment still depend on cross-border supply chains, and the 2020 procurement scramble remains the reference case for how trade friction can slow outbreak response.

Counterview. The strongest case against this reading is that 2026 may mark a healthy consolidation rather than a lasting decline. WHO, Gavi and the Global Fund expanded rapidly after 2020, and the 2025-2026 cuts may force a clearer division of labor, more domestic financing and more regional manufacturing in Africa, India and Latin America. mRNA platforms, cheaper sequencing and AI-supported diagnostics may let smaller teams do work that required much larger institutions in 2019, while most outbreaks, including Ebola clusters, still end without becoming COVID-scale events. If WHO members settle PABS by May 2027 and 2027 replenishments recover, 2026 may look like a reset year rather than the start of a weaker global system.
