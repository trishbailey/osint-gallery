Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history inflection

  1. 1973-1986 Oil-shock and stockpile era

    The 1973 Arab oil embargo and the 1979 Iranian Revolution pushed OECD governments to build strategic stocks, cut fuel use and back new supply from the North Sea, Alaska and Mexico, while the 1986 Saudi production shift then drove prices sharply lower and taught producers that market share could break cartel discipline.

  2. 1986-2003 Cheap-energy globalisation

    Low oil prices through much of 1986-2003 encouraged just-in-time trade and lower security spending, while China’s industrial rise began to pull copper, aluminium and bulk commodities into a larger global cycle.

  3. 2003-2014 China-led commodity boom

    China’s World Trade Organization (WTO)-era industrial expansion after 2001 drove a commodity boom that kept Brent above $100 for sustained periods and pushed miners into large capex programs, but it also deepened dependence on Chinese processing for many minerals.

  4. 2014-2020 Shale reset

    Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC)’s November 2014 decision not to cut output after global supply overtook demand forced a price collapse, cut upstream investment and left a thinner oil project pipeline.

  5. 2025-2026 Middle East supply shock

    The February 2026 Middle East conflict and the September 2026 attacks on Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline pushed Brent to $131 on 2026-09-15 and exposed how oil, sulphur, aluminium and helium disruptions can spread through battery, fertiliser and semiconductor chains.

Where today sits. Inflection: October 2026 sits below the 2008 oil-price peak in nominal terms but above most of 2025, with Brent averaging $114 in September 2026, reaching $131 on 2026-09-15 after the attacks on Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline, and the United States Energy Information Administration (EIA) still seeing $105 in 4Q26. Trajectory and driving forces

Since February 2026, the oil system has been moving from an efficiency-first model toward a security-first model. Saudi Arabia, Iran and OPEC+ sit at different choke points, and the EIA's current outlook has oil prices easing from early-October 2026 levels as bypass routes and repairs recover flows. [3] [4] [2]

  • Middle East transit risk. The Strait of Hormuz, Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline and Red Sea routes carry more than oil, and the September 2026 attacks cut a Saudi bypass that had been moving more than 5.0 million b/d, raised tanker insurance and forced ship-to-ship transfers along Oman’s coast. In September 2026, that bottleneck meant buyers bid for prompt supply, inventories fell and delivered costs rose even when some Saudi barrels could be rerouted.
  • Producer revenue management. OPEC+ governments still want higher fiscal revenue, but the World Bank projects slower long-run oil-demand growth, more diversified production and ample supply capacity over the forecast horizon. The mechanism in 2026 is a shorter pricing horizon, because a long spike can destroy demand and accelerate substitution even when producers want stronger near-term revenue.

Turning points

  1. 1973-10

    Arab oil embargo begins The 1973 embargo converted oil from a traded fuel into a security issue for OECD governments and led to strategic stocks, the 1974 creation of the International Energy Agency (IEA) and a multi-decade policy focus on diversification.

  2. 2014-11

    OPEC keeps output steady as global supply exceeds demand OPEC’s November 2014 decision not to cut turned an oversupplied market into a price collapse, accelerated U.S. shale’s role in market balancing and cut investment across upstream oil. [1]

  3. 2026-02

    Middle East conflict disrupts oil and linked industrial inputs The February 2026 conflict pushed crude above $100 for most of 2Q26 and disrupted aluminium, sulphur and helium flows, showing that hydrocarbon chokepoints can also jam mineral and manufacturing chains. [5] [6]

  4. 2026-09-15

    Attack on Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline pushes Brent to $131 The 2026-09-15 attack proved that even bypass infrastructure outside the Strait of Hormuz can be hit, raising tanker rates, insurance costs and the value of spare inventory and rerouting capacity. [2]

Current phase

October 2026 is a route-risk phase, because EIA data show oil still carries a Middle East route-risk premium, and governments and manufacturers are buying time through inventories and rerouting rather than relying on spot markets alone. [2]

What's at stake.

  • Producers: Revenue that funds state budgets, from Moscow to Riyadh to Tehran.
  • Importers: Prices, supply security and exposure to choke points.

Counterview. The strongest case against a durable security premium is that 2026 may look more like a short conflict shock than a new regime. The EIA said Middle East exports rose in September versus August despite attacks, crude shut-ins fell from 10.9 million b/d in May to 4.8 million b/d in September, and repairs to Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline began restoring flows on 2026-09-22. The World Bank still sees slower oil-demand growth, diversified production and ample supply capacity as downside forces over the forecast horizon.
