Energy, commodities and critical minerals

Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history inflection

  1. 1973-1986 Oil-shock and stockpile era

    The 1973 Arab oil embargo and the 1979 Iranian Revolution pushed OECD governments to build strategic stocks, cut fuel use and back new supply from the North Sea, Alaska and Mexico, while the 1986 Saudi production shift then drove prices sharply lower and taught producers that market share could break cartel discipline.

  2. 1986-2003 Cheap-energy globalisation

    Low oil prices through much of 1986-2003 encouraged just-in-time trade and lower security spending, while China’s industrial rise began to pull copper, aluminium and bulk commodities into a larger global cycle.

  3. 2003-2014 China-led commodity boom

    China’s World Trade Organization (WTO)-era industrial expansion after 2001 drove a commodity boom that kept Brent above $100 for sustained periods and pushed miners into large capex programs, but it also deepened dependence on Chinese processing for many minerals.

  4. 2014-2020 Shale reset and mineral-security revival

    Organization of the Petroleum Exporting Countries (OPEC)’s November 2014 decision not to cut output after global supply overtook demand forced a price collapse, cut upstream investment and left a thinner oil project pipeline, while the United States moved mineral security into formal policy with a federal framework and a 2018 critical-minerals list that named 35 commodities.

  5. 2021-2024 Transition minerals enter energy-security doctrine

    The International Energy Agency (IEA)’s 2021 assessment showed that a typical electric car needs six times the mineral inputs of a conventional car and an onshore wind plant needs nine times more mineral resources than a gas-fired plant, and by April 2024 United Nations (UN) Secretary-General Antonio Guterres had launched a panel on critical energy transition minerals as governments moved from climate targets to supply-chain policy.

  6. 2025-2026 Dual security shock

    In 2025-2026, critical-mineral prices rebounded, with lithium more than doubling and cobalt rising about 130%, while the February 2026 Middle East conflict and the September 2026 attacks on Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline pushed Brent to $131 on 2026-09-15 and exposed how oil, sulphur, aluminium and helium disruptions can spread through battery, fertiliser and semiconductor chains.

Where today sits. Inflection: October 2026 sits below the 2008 oil-price peak in nominal terms but above most of 2025, with Brent averaging $114 in September 2026, reaching $131 on 2026-09-15 after the attacks on Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline, and the United States Energy Information Administration (EIA) still seeing $105 in 4Q26. October 2026 also sits near a high point in critical-mineral concentration, with the average share of the top refined supplier at 70% in 2025 versus 68% in 2020 and Europe’s gallium and heavy rare-earth prices about five times Chinese domestic prices in 2026. 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9

Trajectory and driving forces

Since 2021, and especially since February 2026, the energy system has been moving from an efficiency-first model toward a security-first model. Saudi Arabia, Iran, OPEC+, China, Indonesia and the Democratic Republic of the Congo sit at different choke points, so a shock in one corridor now moves through oil, refined metals, sulphuric acid, batteries and semiconductors rather than staying in one market. The EIA's current outlook has oil prices easing from early-October 2026 levels as bypass routes and repairs recover flows, but the IEA shows that critical-mineral demand kept growing near 10% a year and that refining concentration rose again in 2025. Together these keep a resilience premium in prices today rather than a return to 2010s pricing. 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8

  • Middle East transit risk. The Strait of Hormuz, Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline and Red Sea routes carry more than oil, and the September 2026 attacks cut a Saudi bypass that had been moving more than 5.0 million b/d, raised tanker insurance and forced ship-to-ship transfers along Oman’s coast. In September 2026, that bottleneck meant buyers bid for prompt supply, inventories fell and delivered costs rose even when some Saudi barrels could be rerouted.
  • Mineral intensity of electrification. IEA data show that a typical electric car uses six times the mineral inputs of a conventional car and that global battery demand grew by more than 35% in 2025 to above 1.5 TWh. MIT research shows a different transmission channel from oil, because copper, lithium and rare-earth price shocks raise the cost of building new grids, batteries and turbines rather than the cost of running existing capital.
  • Concentration and export controls. China and Indonesia captured over three quarters of refined-supply growth between 2023 and 2025, while Democratic Republic of the Congo export quotas widened the IEA’s projected cobalt supply gap to above 25% by 2035. The mechanism in 2026 is policy leverage, because dominant refiners or producers can tighten supply, split domestic and overseas prices and deter ex-China investment by making future price formation political as well as commercial.
  • Producer revenue management. OPEC+ governments still want higher fiscal revenue, but the World Bank projects slower long-run oil-demand growth, more diversified production and ample supply capacity over the forecast horizon. The mechanism in 2026 is a shorter pricing horizon, because a long spike can destroy demand and accelerate substitution even when producers want stronger near-term revenue.
  • State-backed localisation and recycling. The United States, the European Union, Japan and IEA members are pushing domestic mining, refining, stockpiles and recycling because supply-security risk moved from theory to procurement. The mechanism is redundancy by design, with governments accepting higher near-term costs in exchange for lower single-country dependence, while average recycling across major energy minerals could rise from about 10% today to close to 20% by 2040 under current policies.

Turning points

  1. 1973-10

    Arab oil embargo begins The 1973 embargo converted oil from a traded fuel into a security issue for OECD governments and led to strategic stocks, the 1974 creation of the IEA and a multi-decade policy focus on diversification.

  2. 2014-11

    OPEC keeps output steady as global supply exceeds demand OPEC’s November 2014 decision not to cut turned an oversupplied market into a price collapse, accelerated U.S. shale’s role in market balancing and cut investment across upstream oil. 1

  3. 2018-05

    United States formalises a modern federal critical-minerals list Executive Order 13817 and the 2018 federal list moved mineral security into industrial policy and created a template for later subsidy, permitting and stockpile measures. 1 2

  4. 2021-05

    IEA publishes The Role of Critical Minerals in Clean Energy Transitions The 2021 IEA report connected EVs, grids and renewables to mineral demand at scale and gave finance ministries and defense planners a quantitative basis for treating copper, lithium, nickel and rare earths as energy-security inputs. 1 2

  5. 2024-04

    United Nations launches the Critical Energy Transition Minerals Panel Antonio Guterres and UNCTAD moved the debate from access alone to access plus benefit-sharing, local value addition and trade rules for producer states. 1

  6. 2026-02

    Middle East conflict disrupts oil and linked industrial inputs The February 2026 conflict pushed crude above $100 for most of 2Q26 and disrupted aluminium, sulphur and helium flows, showing that hydrocarbon chokepoints can also jam mineral and manufacturing chains. 1 2

  7. 2026-09-15

    Attack on Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline pushes Brent to $131 The 2026-09-15 attack proved that even bypass infrastructure outside the Strait of Hormuz can be hit, raising tanker rates, insurance costs and the value of spare inventory and rerouting capacity. 1

Current phase

October 2026 is a dual-premium phase, because EIA data show oil still carries a Middle East route-risk premium while IEA data show copper, lithium, cobalt and strategic minor minerals carry a concentration and export-control premium. In 2026, governments and manufacturers from the United States to Japan are therefore buying time through inventories, rerouting, long-term offtakes and subsidy-backed refining rather than relying on spot markets alone. 1 2 3 4

What's at stake.

  • Producers: Revenue that funds state budgets, from Moscow to Riyadh to Tehran.
  • Importers: Prices, supply security and exposure to choke points.

Links to other storylines

  • Middle East and the Iran axis: Iran-related fighting in 2026 turned oil, sulphur, aluminium and helium into the same operational problem because Gulf shipping routes, bypass pipelines and refinery inputs were hit together.
  • Trade wars, tariffs and supply chains: China, the United States and the European Union now use export controls, local-content rules and subsidy competition to influence who refines cobalt, graphite, rare earths and lithium, so commodity markets are moving into trade-policy portfolios.
  • AI race, compute and chip export controls: Gallium, germanium, helium and electricity demand link mineral security to semiconductors and data-centre expansion, with the IEA now treating high-tech and digital systems as part of the same supply-security problem.
  • Central banks, inflation and debt: EIA oil shocks raise headline inflation and freight costs quickly, while MIT’s capital-formation channel means copper and lithium shocks land later through grids, batteries and industrial capex.

Counterview. The strongest case against a durable security-premium thesis is that 2026 may look more like a short conflict shock than a new commodity regime. The EIA said Middle East exports rose in September versus August despite attacks, crude shut-ins fell from 10.9 million b/d in May to 4.8 million b/d in September, and repairs to Saudi Arabia’s East-West pipeline began restoring flows on 2026-09-22. The World Bank still sees slower oil-demand growth, diversified production and ample supply capacity as downside forces over the forecast horizon, while the IEA says projected copper and lithium balances have improved and rare-earth concentration eased modestly with new United States and Malaysia capacity. If 2027 brings de-escalation plus battery-chemistry shifts away from cobalt and more recycling, 2026 could end up as a temporary spike rather than the start of a longer security-cost cycle.
