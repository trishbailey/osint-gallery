Global governance and alliances

Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history inflection

  1. 1944-1949 Founding architecture

    The United States and 43 other governments used Bretton Woods in July 1944 to create the International Monetary Fund (IMF) and the World Bank, and 50 governments signed the United Nations (UN) Charter in San Francisco in June 1945. The United States, Canada, and 10 European governments then formed North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) in April 1949, establishing a layered order in which universal institutions sat beside a US-led security alliance.

  2. 1950-1989 Cold War dual track

    From 1950 to 1989, the United States and the Soviet Union divided security politics, but the UN, the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade, and the Non-Aligned Movement kept a universal diplomatic lane open. France, West Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom, and the United States created the G6 at Rambouillet in 1975, and Canada joined in 1976, giving advanced economies a smaller steering group outside the UN.

  3. 1990-2007 Post-Cold War expansion

    From 1990 to 2007, the United States operated in a far looser field after the Soviet collapse in 1991, and the World Trade Organization (WTO) entered force in 1995 as trade legalization deepened. NATO and the European Union (EU) enlarged through the 1990s and 2000s, while the Group of Eight (G8) and ad hoc coalitions handled security crises from the Balkans to Iraq, showing that the post-Cold War order mixed universal rules with selective enforcement.

  4. 2008-2013 Group of Twenty (G20) elevation and emerging-power voice

    The 2008 financial crisis cut Western authority on economic management and pushed the G20 leaders' forum, first convened in Washington in November 2008, above the Group of Seven (G7) for crisis coordination. Brazil, Russia, India, China, and South Africa also turned Brazil, Russia, India, China, South Africa and partner economies bloc (BRICS) from an acronym into a diplomatic caucus, and the group later sponsored the New Development Bank in 2014 to widen financing options.

  5. 2014-2021 Return of major-power rivalry

    From 2014 to 2021, Russia's seizure of Crimea in March 2014 returned hard-power rivalry to Europe, and the G8 reverted to the G7. The United States-China contest, disputes over trade and technology, and weak reform at the IMF, the World Bank, the WTO, and the UN Security Council left major institutions with wider agendas but thinner consensus.

  6. 2022-2026 Layered competition and club diplomacy

    From 2022 to 2026, Russia's full-scale invasion of Ukraine in February 2022 gave the G7 a stronger security role, while the UN Security Council again showed the limits of veto-bound diplomacy. BRICS expanded beyond its original five members at the 2024 Kazan summit, and the G20 stayed useful because India, Brazil, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, Indonesia, and other middle powers kept one forum where Washington, Beijing, Moscow, and Brussels still had to bargain.

Where today sits. Inflection: October 2026 gives the G7 more security weight than at any point since Russia left the G8 in 2014, BRICS its largest membership since its 2009 launch, and the G20 the bridge function it first assumed in November 2008. October 2026 still leaves the UN, the WTO and the Bretton Woods bodies at the legal and financial core, but more bargaining has shifted into smaller caucuses than in the 1990s. 1 2 3 4 5 6

Trajectory and driving forces

Since the 2008 financial crisis, global governance has been moving from a mainly universal model toward a layered system in which the G7, BRICS+, the G20, regional bodies, development banks, and ad hoc coalitions divide labor by issue. The Russia-Ukraine war since 2022 has tightened Western coordination inside the G7, BRICS expansion in 2024 has given China, Russia, India, Brazil, South Africa, Egypt, Ethiopia, Iran, Saudi Arabia, and the United Arab Emirates a larger platform, and middle powers have used the G20 to resist a formal two-bloc split. By 2026, the contest is less about abolishing the 1945 architecture than about who gets agenda-setting power inside and around it. 1 2 3 4 5 6 7

  • Shift in economic weight. China, India, Gulf states, and African members now command more trade, energy, and capital than their voting weight in 1945-era bodies reflects, so BRICS+ and G20 coalitions press the IMF, World Bank, and UN Security Council for quota, leadership, and seat reform.
  • Great-power rivalry. US-China rivalry and Russia-West conflict make consensus harder in the UN Security Council, the WTO, and treaty talks, so Washington uses the G7 and allied formats more heavily while Beijing and Moscow invest more effort in BRICS and other non-Western caucuses.
  • Middle-power leverage. India, Brazil, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, Indonesia, Turkey, and the United Arab Emirates gain bargaining room by joining several clubs at once, and their hedging keeps the G20 alive because neither Washington nor Beijing can claim them as automatic votes.
  • Sanctions and payment risk. US and EU sanctions on Russia after 2022 raised the value of alternative settlement channels for China, India, Gulf producers, and other states, which is why BRICS keeps pushing local-currency trade even though the dollar retains the deepest markets and payment network.
  • Issue overload. Climate finance, pandemic rules, artificial intelligence (AI) standards, debt workouts, and carbon border measures put very different issues into one governance queue, and governments then move parts of the workload into issue-specific coalitions or regional forums when universal negotiations stall.

Turning points

  1. 1944-07

    Bretton Woods conference creates the IMF and the World Bank The United States and its partners built postwar financial governance around institutions that tied exchange-rate stability, development finance, and US economic weight into one framework. 1 2

  2. 1945-06

    UN Charter is signed in San Francisco The UN gave universal membership a legal center, but the Security Council veto ensured that major-power consent would remain the ceiling on enforcement. 1 2

  3. 1975-11

    The Rambouillet summit launches the G6, which becomes the G7 in 1976 France, West Germany, Italy, Japan, the United Kingdom, the United States, and then Canada created a smaller leaders' club because oil, inflation, and monetary shocks moved faster than universal diplomacy. 1 2

  4. 1995-01

    The WTO enters force The WTO marked the high tide of rule-dense globalization by giving trade law a stronger dispute framework and a broader policy reach than the old GATT system. 1 2

  5. 2008-11

    The first G20 Leaders' Summit meets in Washington The United States and other major economies accepted that crisis management after the 2008 crash required China, India, Brazil, Saudi Arabia, South Africa, and other large non-G7 actors at the top table. 1 2

  6. 2014-03

    Russia seizes Crimea and leaves the G8 framework Russia's 2014 move ended the post-Cold War idea that the G8 could socialize Moscow into the Western club, and it restored the G7 as the core caucus of advanced democracies. 1 2 3

  7. 2022-02-24

    Russia launches its full-scale invasion of Ukraine The 2022 war strengthened G7 and NATO coordination, exposed the UN Security Council's constraints, and widened the gap between Western and non-Western readings of order and intervention. 1 2

  8. 2024-10

    The Kazan BRICS+ summit formalizes expansion beyond the original five members The 2024 expansion gave emerging and middle powers a larger caucus for contesting Western institutional privilege, even as internal differences inside BRICS+ limited tight bloc discipline. 1 2

Current phase

October 2026 is a phase of competitive plurilateralism: the UN, IMF, World Bank, and WTO still supply legality, finance, and technical process, but agenda-setting is being contested by caucuses such as the G7, BRICS+, and issue-based coalitions. India, Brazil, South Africa, Indonesia, Saudi Arabia, and the EU now matter because they can move between these tables. 1 2 3

Historical analogue (counter): Non-Aligned Movement under late Cold War pressure, 1961-1983

Why comparable. From 1961 to 1983, India, Egypt, Yugoslavia, and Indonesia used superpower rivalry to widen bargaining space and resist formal alignment.

Where it breaks. The 2026 field is more integrated through the G20, IMF, WTO, and global capital markets than the Cold War field of 1961.

What happened then. The Non-Aligned Movement won diplomatic room on decolonization and development, but Washington and Moscow still set most security ceilings. 1 2

Links to other storylines

  • Russia-Ukraine and European security: Russia's February 2022 invasion raised the G7's security role, hardened sanctions coordination across the United States, the EU, Japan, Canada, and the United Kingdom, and reduced room for UN Security Council action on European security.
  • US-China rivalry and Taiwan: US-China rivalry pushes governance fights into export controls, investment screening, maritime law, and technology standards, which makes the G20 and many UN bodies harder to use for broad bargains.
  • Trade wars, tariffs and supply chains: WTO strain, tariff bargaining, carbon border measures, and supply-chain industrial policy move rule-making from universal trade law toward bilateral, regional, and club-based deals.
  • Global health and biosecurity: Pandemic treaty bargaining and vaccine-access disputes show that health governance now turns on finance, intellectual property, and North-South trust as much as on World Health Organization (WHO) procedure.

Counterview. The stronger case against a structural break starts with power asymmetry in 2026: the United States, the EU, Japan, and the United Kingdom still anchor the deepest capital markets, the reserve currency, major aid budgets, and most high-end security alliances. BRICS+ also contains India and China, Saudi Arabia and Iran, and both energy exporters and large importers with different threat pictures, so the group may stay a bargaining club rather than become an alternative governing core. If the G20 keeps issuing joint texts through 2027 and IMF or World Bank reforms stay incremental, the 2024-2026 tension may look like hard bargaining inside an order that is adapting, not breaking.
