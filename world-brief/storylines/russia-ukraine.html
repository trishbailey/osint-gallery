Russia-Ukraine and European security

Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history inflection

  1. 1991-2004 Post-Soviet settlement with weak enforcement

    In 1991-2004, Ukraine left the Soviet Union, inherited the world’s third-largest nuclear arsenal, and signed the 1994 Budapest Memorandum with Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States in exchange for security assurances that were political rather than automatic military guarantees.

  2. 2004-2013 Competing security alignments

    Between the 2004 Orange Revolution and Viktor Yanukovych’s 2013 refusal to sign the European Union (EU) Association Agreement, Kyiv tried to balance EU ties, North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) ambitions and Russian pressure, while Moscow treated Ukraine as central to its western security position.

  3. 2014-2021 Crimea annexation and Donbas limited war

    In February-March 2014, Russia took control of Crimea and backed armed formations in Donetsk and Luhansk, while the 2014-2015 Minsk agreements reduced but did not settle fighting that had killed more than 14,000 people before February 2022.

  4. 2022 Full-scale invasion and failed decapitation attempt

    On 24 February 2022, Vladimir Putin ordered a full-scale invasion intended in part to topple Volodymyr Zelensky’s government, but Ukrainian resistance and Western support prevented a seizure of Kyiv and forced a Russian withdrawal from northern Ukraine by April 2022.

  5. 2022-2024 Attrition war and European military adaptation

    From September 2022 through 2024, Russia entrenched control over parts of four Ukrainian regions, NATO refocused on the eastern flank, Finland joined NATO in 2023, and Europe moved from emergency support toward larger munitions, air-defense and force-posture plans.

  6. 2025-2026 European-led deterrence debate and ceasefire probing

    By 2025-2026, Donald Trump’s push for negotiations, Russia’s continued claims over all of Donbas, and European plans for longer-term rearmament shifted debate from outright military victory to whether a monitored ceasefire could coexist with years of armed deterrence.

Where today sits. Inflection: October 2026 sits well below 24 February 2022, when Vladimir Putin ordered the full-scale invasion and Russian forces tried to seize Kyiv before withdrawing from northern Ukraine by April 2022. October 2026 also sits above the 2015 Minsk period in European militarization, because the 2025-2026 debate has shifted toward monitored ceasefire options alongside multi-year deterrence and rearmament. Trajectory and driving forces

Since 2022, the path has pointed toward armed coexistence rather than a full settlement, with Ukraine moving deeper into the European security system, Russia preparing for a long confrontation with NATO and the EU, and the United States under Donald Trump pressing Europeans to carry more of the burden while exploring ceasefire terms. [7] [8] [9] [10] [11] [3] [12]

  • Russian security aims and bargaining position. Vladimir Putin wants a buffer on Russia’s western border and limits on Kyiv’s military and institutional choices, so Moscow can accept a pause in 2026 more easily than a settlement that leaves Ukraine fully armed, Western-backed and politically independent.
  • Ukrainian state resilience and westward integration. Volodymyr Zelensky and the Ukrainian state seek survival, reconstruction and deeper EU and NATO ties, and every year since 2014 has made political reintegration with Moscow less acceptable in Kyiv, which narrows the space for a grand bargain.
  • European rearmament and institutional adaptation. NATO, the EU and states such as Poland, Germany, France and the United Kingdom are raising spending on munitions, air defense and eastern-flank posture because Russia’s 2022 invasion exposed stockpile, readiness and command gaps that cannot be closed in one budget cycle.
  • U.S. policy volatility. Donald Trump’s 2025-2026 pressure for negotiations changes timing because Moscow tests whether Washington will reduce support first, while European governments try to prevent a deterrence gap before their own production lines and troop posture expand.

Turning points

  1. 1994-12

    Ukraine signed the Budapest Memorandum with Russia, the United Kingdom and the United States. The 1994 deal traded more than 1,700 nuclear warheads for assurances without automatic collective defense, which later shaped Kyiv’s threat perception and Western debates over obligations. [4]

  2. 2014-02

    Viktor Yanukovych fled Kyiv after the Maidan uprising. The February 2014 collapse of Yanukovych’s government convinced the Kremlin that political leverage in Ukraine was failing and that force could become the instrument for blocking Kyiv’s westward turn. [1] [2] [13]

  3. 2014-03

    Russia annexed Crimea after taking control of the peninsula. The March 2014 move broke the post-1991 territorial settlement in Europe and gave Russia a stronger military position in the Black Sea while pushing Ukraine toward deeper Western alignment. [1] [2] [13]

  4. 2015-02

    Russia, Ukraine, France and Germany agreed Minsk II in Belarus. The February 2015 package lowered combat in parts of Donbas but left sovereignty, enforcement and sequencing disputes unresolved, which turned the line of contact into a suspended war rather than a settlement. [2] [4]

  5. 2022-02-24

    Russia launched a full-scale invasion of Ukraine. The 24 February 2022 attack ended the idea of a cooperative European security order with Russia and forced NATO, the EU and Ukraine into a long-war posture. [1] [5]

  6. 2022-04

    Russian forces withdrew from the Kyiv axis and northern Ukraine. The April 2022 withdrawal showed that Ukraine could deny Russia a rapid regime-change campaign, which shifted the war toward attrition, mobilization and industrial endurance. [1]

  7. 2023-04

    Finland joined NATO. Finland’s April 2023 accession lengthened the NATO-Russia border and turned the war from a dispute over Ukraine alone into a wider revision of northern and eastern European defense planning. [3] [6]

  8. 2025-01

    Donald Trump returned to the White House and reopened pressure for a negotiated end-state while urging Europe to assume more defense responsibility. The January 2025 political change in Washington accelerated European planning for allied-led defense because U.S. military support and nuclear assurance looked less automatic than in 2022-2024. [11] [14] [12]

Current phase

The current phase in late 2026 is ceasefire probing inside a still-active attrition war, with Ukraine, Russia, NATO and the EU all preparing for a confrontation that most planners are organising around as outlasting any first truce. [11] [15] [12]

What's at stake.

  • Ukraine: Its territory, its sovereignty and whether its security rests on Western guarantees or on its own arms.
  • Russia: Control of occupied land and limits on Kyiv's alignment, set against the fiscal cost of the war.
  • European governments: Their own security and a growing share of the bill as United States (US) support shrinks.
  • United States: Its leverage over a settlement and the cost of continued support.

The tensions.

  • Europe vs Washington: European governments are paying more while Washington shapes the talks, so who pays and who negotiates are diverging.
  • Russia's demands vs its finances: Moscow still demands all of Donbas while its oil and gas revenues have fallen 17% this year.

Historical analogue: Korean armistice model, 1951-1953

Why comparable. The Korean case matches a mechanism in which outside powers back local combatants, front lines stabilize before a political settlement, and an armistice separates battlefield control from legal peace.

Where it breaks. Ukraine in 2026 faces a far longer frontier, deeper drone and missile strike reach, and a direct EU accession track that Korea in 1953 did not have.

What happened then. The July 1953 armistice stopped large-scale combat without a peace treaty and turned deterrence, troop presence and alliance guarantees into the real structure of stability for decades. [15]

Links to other storylines

  • Global governance and alliances: NATO and EU decisions in 2026-2027 on burden-sharing, sanctions, accession and reassurance forces will determine whether a ceasefire becomes a stable security regime or only a pause.
  • US domestic politics and policy direction: Donald Trump’s 2025-2027 choices on weapons, troop posture and alliance commitments are a direct input into Russian and European calculations about timing and risk.
  • Energy and commodities: Russian strikes on Ukraine’s grid, Black Sea access and European diversification plans keep the war tied to electricity prices, gas strategy and reconstruction finance through 2027.
  • Cyber conflict and information operations: Russian coercive activity below open war, including cyber operations and sabotage in Europe, gives Moscow a way to pressure NATO members in 2026-2027 without crossing the Article 5 threshold.

Counterview. A stronger alternative reading says Donald Trump, Vladimir Putin and Volodymyr Zelensky could still reach a wider bargain in 2027 because Russia faces mounting manpower and budget strain after 2026, while Ukraine’s longer-range strike reach has improved enough to make a continued offensive less attractive to Moscow.
