Trade wars, tariffs and supply chains

Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history inflection

  1. 1930-1947 Protectionist spiral and rollback

    US President Herbert Hoover signed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act in 1930, 10 trading partners retaliated, and VOA says US exports fell 66% before President Franklin Roosevelt replaced the system with bilateral deals in 1934.

  2. 1947-1994 Rules-based tariff reduction

    The United States and 22 other economies signed GATT in 1947, and the post-war tariff rounds gave US, European and Japanese firms a more stable base for cross-border production and shipping.

  3. 1995-2016 WTO discipline and China-scale globalization

    The World Trade Organization entered into force in 1995, China joined in 2001, and manufacturers in electronics, machinery and consumer goods built long supply chains around Chinese coastal export hubs and just-in-time logistics.

  4. 2018-2020 Tariffs return as great-power tool

    US President Donald Trump used Section 232 in March 2018 for 25% steel and 10% aluminum duties and used Section 301 for successive China tariffs that reached 25% on $200 billion of imports on 2019-05-10. China answered with countermeasures, and the 2020 Phase One deal froze escalation without removing most tariffs.

  5. 2020-2024 Shock stacking and China+1

    COVID-19, the semiconductor shortage and later security disputes pushed boards in 2020-2024 to add Vietnam, India, Mexico and other sites while keeping part of their China base. The European Union and the United States also moved some allied disputes into managed arrangements, including the 2021 pause in steel and aluminum retaliation.

  6. 2025-2026 Tariffs as standing operating cost

    KPMG reported US imports jumped more than 50% in Q1 2025 as firms front-loaded shipments before new duties, and Thomson Reuters found 76% of trade professionals in 2026 anticipated the US tariff approach to last at least four years. KPMG also reported that a 2026 Supreme Court decision cut off one emergency-power route, but the White House restored most tariffs through Section 122 for 150 days and kept Section 301 and Section 232 available.

Where today sits. Inflection: October 2026 sits below a trade collapse, with the WTO still projecting 1.9% merchandise trade growth in 2026 after 4.6% in 2025. Trajectory and driving forces

Since 2018, Washington has kept tariffs, export controls and origin rules in play across China and, in 2025-2026, across more partners, which has pushed boards in Detroit, Shenzhen and Stuttgart to treat trade policy as a factory-location variable rather than a customs issue. McKinsey reported in 2026 that US imports from China fell across consumer electronics while ASEAN and India gained share, even as China kept supplying more intermediate goods to Asian partners. Thomson Reuters reported in 2026 that 68% of trade professionals put supply-chain management first and 39% said their firms were absorbing at least part of tariff costs, which means the adjustment is now running through margins, capex and consumer prices at the same time. 1 2 3 4 5 6 7

  • US tariff persistence after legal rerouting. KPMG reported that a 2026 Supreme Court decision blocked one emergency-power basis, but the White House restored most tariffs through Section 122 for 150 days and could reopen Section 301 and Section 232 tracks, so companies still have to price US tariff exposure into 2027 plans.
  • Corporate redesign from lowest cost to resilience. MIT's Yossi Sheffi and Thomson Reuters describe companies remapping sub-tier suppliers, rewriting contracts and changing plant footprints, while Thomson Reuters found supply-chain management rose to 68% from 35% in one year.
  • Regionalization rather than trade collapse. The WTO still projects 1.9% merchandise trade growth in 2026, and McKinsey says ASEAN and India gained US electronics share while China kept a larger role in intermediate goods sent into Asia, so trade is being rerouted more than shut down.
  • Compliance and origin enforcement. KPMG reported that US Customs and Border Protection recovered $235 million in 2025 through close to 500 audits versus $118 million in just over 400 audits the year before, which makes origin proof and tariff classification as important as wage differences for many importers.
  • Consumer and margin politics. Supply Chain Management Review found 74% of shoppers across seven economies were hit by higher prices, while Thomson Reuters found 39% of trade professionals were absorbing or considering absorbing tariff costs, so executives now choose directly between market share and margins.

Turning points

  1. 1930-06-17

    US President Herbert Hoover signed the Smoot-Hawley Tariff Act. The 1930 law raised duties on nearly 2,000 imports and triggered retaliation from Canada, France and others, showing that broad tariffs can damage exports as fast as they protect domestic producers. 1 2

  2. 1947-10-30

    The United States and 22 economies signed the General Agreement on Tariffs and Trade. The 1947 agreement replaced interwar retaliation with negotiated tariff rounds and gave investors in North America, Europe and Japan a more stable rule set for cross-border manufacturing. 1

  3. 1995-01-01

    The World Trade Organization entered into force. The 1995 WTO added dispute settlement and wider rule coverage, which made long multi-country supply chains more bankable for manufacturers and lenders. 1 2

  4. 2001-12-11

    China joined the World Trade Organization. China's 2001 entry gave multinationals a very large lower-cost manufacturing base and accelerated single-country concentration in electronics, machinery and consumer goods. 1 2

  5. 2018-03-08

    US President Donald Trump imposed Section 232 tariffs on steel and aluminum. The March 2018 action revived national-security tariffs, widened trade friction from China to allies in Europe and North America, and raised input costs for downstream manufacturers. 1 2

  6. 2019-05-10

    The United States raised tariffs from 10% to 25% on $200 billion of Chinese imports. The May 2019 increase showed the dispute had moved from bargaining to escalation, and companies started more serious China+1 sourcing, stockpiling and contract revision. 1 2

  7. 2025-04-02

    Washington announced a wider 2025 tariff push across multiple partners. KPMG reported firms rushed goods into US ports after the April 2025 announcements, with imports up more than 50% in Q1 2025, proving that tariff announcements alone can redirect shipping and inventory timing. 1

  8. 2026

    A 2026 Supreme Court ruling cut off one emergency-power basis for US tariffs, and the White House shifted to other tariff authorities. KPMG reported that Section 122 restored most tariffs for 150 days and that Section 301 and Section 232 investigations were likely next, so the central question became legal durability rather than whether tariffs would disappear. 1

Current phase

October 2026 is a phase of managed fragmentation. WTO trade volumes are still growing, but KPMG and Thomson Reuters show that boards now treat tariffs, origin rules and compliance audits as standing costs, so Mexico, Vietnam, India and US locations gain weight even when global demand stays positive. 1 2 3 4

What's at stake.

  • US importers and consumers: Higher costs, partly absorbed by firms and partly passed on.
  • Exporting economies: Market access and factory investment that tariffs are moving between countries.
  • US Treasury: Customs revenue that court rulings and refunds are cutting.

Historical analogue: U.S.-Japan auto and semiconductor friction, 1981-1994

Why comparable. Washington used trade pressure in the 1980s to move Japanese production toward the United States, and current US tariffs are again making foreign firms buy market access with local assembly.

Where it breaks. China in 2026 is a much larger manufacturing system than Japan in 1985, and current disputes cover sub-tier components, chips, data and origin rules rather than finished cars alone.

What happened then. Japanese firms answered the 1980s conflict with export restraints, US investment and local supply chains, which softened bilateral deficits without restoring a pre-1970s US factory model. 1 2

Links to other storylines

  • AI race, compute and chip export controls: US tariff waivers, export controls and AI-related electronics demand now move together, so chip policy and customs policy are increasingly one planning problem for TSMC, Samsung and Nvidia.
  • China's economy and global effects: China's 2026 growth model depends more heavily on exports and Asian intermediate-goods trade, so every US tariff revision feeds back into Chinese factory utilization and domestic substitution programs.
  • Central banks, inflation and debt: The Federal Reserve, the ECB and the Bank of England must separate tariff-driven goods inflation from weak demand, because firms are still passing through part of the duty bill to households in 2026.
  • Energy, commodities and critical minerals: The WTO says a 2026 oil and LNG shock tied to the Middle East can cut merchandise trade growth from 1.9% to 1.4%, so energy security now sits inside trade planning.

Counterview. WTO data for 2025-2026 and company practice give a strong case that tariffs are being overstated as a force on real production. The WTO still sees 1.9% merchandise growth in 2026 after 4.6% in 2025, Supply Chain Management Review argues modern networks are built to keep goods moving, and courts could still narrow US measures enough by 2027 that many firms prefer paperwork changes, tariff engineering and price adjustments over new factories. On this view, 2026 is less a new trade order than a noisy tax-and-compliance cycle layered onto globalization.
