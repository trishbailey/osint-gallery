Africa and the Sahel

Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history inflection

  1. 1960s-1990s Post-independence fragility under ecological pressure

    After the 1960s independences of Mali, Niger, Chad, Mauritania and Burkina Faso, fast population growth and the droughts of the 1970s and 1980s put repeated pressure on farming and pastoral systems across a 5,900-km belt from Senegal to Sudan.

  2. 1990s-2011 Militant southward movement and borderland commercialization

    After the Algerian civil war of the 1990s, GSPC units moved into northern Mali and neighboring borderlands, AQIM was formally created in January 2007, and kidnapping and smuggling turned remote districts in Mali, Mauritania and southern Algeria into revenue zones.

  3. 2012-2019 The Mali crisis becomes a regional war

    The 2012 collapse in northern Mali and France's January 2013 intervention were followed by the G5 Sahel in 2014 and the Multinational Joint Task Force in 2015, yet militant Islamist violence spread from Mali into Burkina Faso, Niger and the Lake Chad basin.

  4. 2020-2023 Coups turn security failure into a governing formula

    Between 18 August 2020 and July 2023, Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger replaced elected governments with military authorities, while Guinea, Chad, Sudan and Gabon showed that the wider belt from Conakry to Khartoum had entered a new cycle of unconstitutional power seizures.

  5. 2023-2025 Regional rupture and partner realignment

    The 15 April 2023 war in Sudan and the September 2023 Alliance of Sahel States between Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger created two linked shocks: an eastern war that destabilized Darfur and Chad, and a western bloc that cut security ties with France, sought Russian backing and completed its break with ECOWAS in early 2025.

  6. 2026-present Military consolidation meets battlefield and internal limits

    In 2026, JNIM and Islamic State Sahel Province kept pressure on Malian, Burkinabè and Nigerien forces, Armed Conflict Location and Event Data project (ACLED) data cited by Carnegie showed battles involving Russian fighters in Mali falling from 537 in 2024 to 24 per month in 2026, and forces loyal to Abdourahamane Tiani needed about 200 Russia-linked personnel to retake Niamey's Air Base 101 after the 29 August 2026 mutiny.

Where today sits. Inflection: October 2026 sits after the end of the France-led and U.S.-supported security architecture that dominated Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger from 2013 to 2022, but before the Alliance of Sahel States (AES)-Russia model has restored territorial control. Militant Islamist violence had already exceeded 23,000 deaths across Africa in 2023, with more than 80% in the Sahel and Somalia, and Abdourahamane Tiani’s forces needed about 200 Russia-linked personnel to retake Niamey’s Air Base 101 after the 29 August 2026 mutiny. Trajectory and driving forces

Since the July 2023 removal of Mohamed Bazoum in Niamey, Assimi Goïta in Bamako, Ibrahim Traoré in Ouagadougou and Abdourahamane Tiani in Niamey have moved from separate military transitions toward a shared AES political project. Since 2024, Russia has replaced France and much of the former U.S. security role in the AES core, but the August 2026 fighting at Air Base 101 and the 2026 decline in battles involving Russian fighters in Mali suggest a regime-protection model rather than a territory-recovery model. Since the 15 April 2023 war in Sudan, Chad and the eastern Sahel have faced heavier refugee, arms-trafficking and diplomatic pressure, while Benin, Togo and Côte d'Ivoire have hardened northern borders to stop the conflict belt moving to the Gulf of Guinea. [4] [11] [5] [10] [7] [12] [1] [8] [13] [14] [15] [16] [9] [17] [18]

  • Regime legitimacy deficit. The coups in Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger gained traction because Ibrahim Boubacar Keïta, Roch Marc Christian Kaboré and Mohamed Bazoum were blamed for insecurity and poor governance, and the current military authorities resist quick elections because elections by 2027 would test their security record and threaten officer patronage networks.
  • Insurgent adaptation. JNIM and Islamic State Sahel Province benefit when Malian, Burkinabè and Nigerien forces protect capitals, air bases and convoy routes first, because insurgents can mass fighters across borders, choose weaker garrisons in Gao, Djibo or Tillabéri, and turn every abusive sweep into new recruitment.
  • External partner substitution with limited capacity. France closed much of its former Sahel posture between 2022 and 2025, the United States lost access to its Niger drone base, and Russia became the anchor security partner for the AES, but SIPRI data cited in 2026 put Russian arms exports 64 percent below their 2015-2019 level, which limits Moscow's ability to fund large territorial rollback campaigns.
  • Institutional rupture with ECOWAS. The AES gives Bamako, Ouagadougou and Niamey a political frame to resist ECOWAS pressure and coordinate border policy, but the same break has weakened access to ECOWAS mediation, trade facilitation and financial credibility, while the confederation still relies on the CFA franc and has not built a separate monetary system.
  • Climate, food and fiscal pressure. The Sahel had about 80 million people in 2018, annual rainfall ranges from roughly 100 mm in the north to 1,000 mm in the south, and 2026 fertilizer and grain shocks linked to Hormuz and Black Sea disruptions are raising import bills for food-dependent governments from Mauritania to Sudan at the same time that International Monetary Fund (IMF) regional growth for sub-Saharan Africa is set at 4.3 percent in 2026.

Turning points

  1. 2007-01

    Al-Qaeda in the Islamic Maghreb is formed from the GSPC. The January 2007 rebranding linked Sahelian smuggling corridors to a wider jihadist organization, improved fundraising through kidnappings, and made northern Mali, southern Algeria and Mauritania part of one operational theatre. [2]

  2. 2012-03

    Mali's state collapse and Bamako coup open the central Sahel front. The 2012 shock showed that northern Mali could be seized faster than Bamako could reinforce it, and Burkina Faso and Niger then faced spillover along already thinly governed borders. [2]

  3. 2013-01

    France launches Operation Serval in Mali. The January 2013 intervention stopped a southward militant push toward Bamako, but it also made external airpower and intelligence the main pillar of Sahel security for the next decade. [2]

  4. 2014-02

    The G5 Sahel and later the 2015 Multinational Joint Task Force formalize regional counterinsurgency. The 2014-2015 institutions recognized that Mali, Niger, Burkina Faso, Chad, Cameroon, Nigeria and Benin could not contain cross-border armed groups with national armies alone. [3]

  5. 2020-08-18

    Colonel Assimi Goïta removes Ibrahim Boubacar Keïta in Mali. The 18 August 2020 coup turned security failure into a repeatable political script for officers in Bamako, Ouagadougou and Niamey. [4]

  6. 2023-04-15

    War begins between the Sudanese Armed Forces (SAF) and Rapid Support Forces (RSF) in Sudan. The Khartoum conflict opened an east-west arc of refugee flows, arms traffic and diplomatic rivalry affecting Darfur, Chad and the wider Sahel at the same time that western Sahel states were severing old partnerships. [6] [7]

  7. 2023-09-16

    Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger sign the Alliance of Sahel States charter. The September 2023 pact converted three national military transitions into a confederal project built around mutual defence, anti-ECOWAS solidarity and new ties with Russia. [5]

  8. 2026-08-29

    Forces loyal to Abdourahamane Tiani defeat a major mutiny in Niamey. The 29 August 2026 fighting showed that even the newest AES government faces internal officer dissent and that Russia-linked support can preserve a capital without solving wider insecurity. [10]

Current phase

In October 2026, the current phase is AES confederal consolidation under battlefield stress in Bamako, Ouagadougou and Niamey. Assimi Goïta, Ibrahim Traoré and Abdourahamane Tiani are building institutions and external partnerships faster than their armies are recovering districts in northern Mali, northern Burkina Faso and western Niger. [5] [10] [13] [9]

Links to other storylines

  • Migration and displacement: Sudan's war and Sahelian rural displacement feed movement through Chad, Libya, Algeria and Tunisia toward the central Mediterranean, and every border closure in Niger or Benin redistributes routes rather than ending them.
  • Climate, food and water security: Low-rainfall seasons, pasture loss and fertilizer shocks raise recruitment pools in Mopti, Tillabéri and northern Burkina Faso more directly than ideology alone, because household survival and armed mobilization are linked at village level.
  • Global governance and alliances: The ECOWAS-AES rupture and United Nations (UN) Security Council splits over UNOWAS reporting limit coordinated sanctions, mediation and peace-support options for West Africa after 2025.
  • Trade wars, tariffs and supply chains: Hormuz and Black Sea disruptions raise fertilizer, fuel and wheat costs for import-dependent treasuries in Mauritania, Mali, Niger and Sudan, and those price shocks make smuggling corridors more valuable.

Counterview. The strongest counterview is that 2026 looks worse than 2024 because Mali, Burkina Faso and Niger are dismantling one security architecture before a new one becomes effective. France's 2013-2022 approach did not stop violence spreading from Kidal to Djibo and Tillabéri, so judging Assimi Goïta, Ibrahim Traoré and Abdourahamane Tiani before an AES force and new procurement cycles are in place by 2027 may understate their room to improve, especially if local ceasefires cut killings without a formal democratic transition.
