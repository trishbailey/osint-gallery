Critical minerals

Rare earths: what feeds into what

Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history inflection

  1. 2003-2014 China-led commodity boom

    China’s World Trade Organization (WTO)-era industrial expansion after 2001 pushed miners into large capex programs, but it also deepened dependence on Chinese processing for many minerals.

  2. 2014-2020 Mineral-security revival

    The United States moved mineral security into formal policy with a federal framework and a 2018 critical-minerals list that named 35 commodities.

  3. 2021-2024 Transition minerals enter energy-security doctrine

    The IEA’s 2021 assessment showed that a typical electric car needs six times the mineral inputs of a conventional car and an onshore wind plant needs nine times more mineral resources than a gas-fired plant, and by April 2024 United Nations (UN) Secretary-General Antonio Guterres had launched a panel on critical energy transition minerals as governments moved from climate targets to supply-chain policy.

  4. 2025-2026 Price rebound and concentration

    In 2025-2026, critical-mineral prices rebounded, with lithium more than doubling and cobalt rising about 130%.

Where today sits. Inflection: October 2026 sits near a high point in critical-mineral concentration, with the average share of the top refined supplier at 70% in 2025 versus 68% in 2020 and Europe’s gallium and heavy rare-earth prices about five times Chinese domestic prices in 2026. 1 2 3 4 5 6 7

Trajectory and driving forces

Since 2021, governments have treated critical minerals as an energy-security input. China, Indonesia and the Democratic Republic of the Congo sit at different choke points, and the IEA shows that critical-mineral demand kept growing near 10% a year and that refining concentration rose again in 2025. 1 2 3 4 5

  • Mineral intensity of electrification. IEA data show that a typical electric car uses six times the mineral inputs of a conventional car and that global battery demand grew by more than 35% in 2025 to above 1.5 TWh. MIT research shows a different transmission channel from oil, because copper, lithium and rare-earth price shocks raise the cost of building new grids, batteries and turbines rather than the cost of running existing capital.
  • Concentration and export controls. China and Indonesia captured over three quarters of refined-supply growth between 2023 and 2025, while Democratic Republic of the Congo export quotas widened the IEA’s projected cobalt supply gap to above 25% by 2035. The mechanism in 2026 is policy leverage, because dominant refiners or producers can tighten supply, split domestic and overseas prices and deter ex-China investment by making future price formation political as well as commercial.
  • State-backed localisation and recycling. The United States, the European Union, Japan and IEA members are pushing domestic mining, refining, stockpiles and recycling because supply-security risk moved from theory to procurement. The mechanism is redundancy by design, with governments accepting higher near-term costs in exchange for lower single-country dependence, while average recycling across major energy minerals could rise from about 10% today to close to 20% by 2040 under current policies.

Turning points

  1. 2018-05

    United States formalises a modern federal critical-minerals list Executive Order 13817 and the 2018 federal list moved mineral security into industrial policy and created a template for later subsidy, permitting and stockpile measures. 1 2

  2. 2021-05

    IEA publishes The Role of Critical Minerals in Clean Energy Transitions The 2021 IEA report connected EVs, grids and renewables to mineral demand at scale and gave finance ministries and defense planners a quantitative basis for treating copper, lithium, nickel and rare earths as energy-security inputs. 1 2

  3. 2024-04

    United Nations launches the Critical Energy Transition Minerals Panel Antonio Guterres and UNCTAD moved the debate from access alone to access plus benefit-sharing, local value addition and trade rules for producer states. 1

Current phase

October 2026 is a concentration phase, because IEA data show copper, lithium, cobalt and strategic minor minerals carry a concentration and export-control premium, and governments and manufacturers from the United States to Japan are buying time through long-term offtakes and subsidy-backed refining. 1 2 3

What's at stake.

  • Refining states: Leverage over the supply and price of processed minerals.
  • Manufacturers: Input costs and exposure to export controls.

The tensions.

  • Security vs efficiency: Governments are paying for domestic refining, stockpiles and recycling that the cheapest supply chain would not hold.

Links to other storylines

  • Trade wars, tariffs and supply chains: China, the United States and the European Union now use export controls, local-content rules and subsidy competition to influence who refines cobalt, graphite, rare earths and lithium, so commodity markets are moving into trade-policy portfolios.
  • AI race, compute and chip export controls: Gallium, germanium, helium and electricity demand link mineral security to semiconductors and data-centre expansion, with the IEA now treating high-tech and digital systems as part of the same supply-security problem.
  • Central banks, inflation and debt: MIT’s capital-formation channel means copper and lithium shocks land later through grids, batteries and industrial capex.
  • Energy and commodities: The February 2026 conflict disrupted aluminium, sulphur and helium flows, showing that hydrocarbon chokepoints can also jam mineral and manufacturing chains.

Counterview. The IEA says projected copper and lithium balances have improved and rare-earth concentration eased modestly with new United States and Malaysia capacity. If 2027 brings battery-chemistry shifts away from cobalt and more recycling, 2025-2026 could end up as a temporary price spike rather than the start of a longer security-cost cycle.
