United States (US)-China rivalry and Taiwan

Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history on trend

  1. 1949-1958 Civil war freeze and early U.S. commitment

    From 1949 to 1958, Mao Zedong's PRC and Chiang Kai-shek's ROC turned the Chinese Civil War into a Strait standoff, with the Korean War, the 1954 U.S.-ROC defense treaty, and the 1954-55 and 1958 Taiwan Strait crises tying Washington directly to Taiwan's defense.

  2. 1971-1982 Diplomatic reversal with guarded deterrence

    From 1971 to 1982, Richard Nixon's 1972 Shanghai Communiqué, Jimmy Carter's 1979 recognition of Beijing, the 1979 Taiwan Relations Act, and Ronald Reagan's 1982 Six Assurances created the U.S. formula of unofficial ties to Taipei plus deterrence against force.

  3. 1995-1996 Missile-crisis proof of concept

    From 1995 to 1996, Lee Teng-hui's U.S. visit and Taiwan's first direct presidential election brought PLA missile tests and two U.S. carrier deployments, showing that cross-Strait disputes could pull Washington and Beijing into acute crisis within weeks.

  4. 2008-2015 Cross-Strait easing without settlement

    From 2008 to 2015, Ma Ying-jeou's government in Taipei and Hu Jintao's and then Xi Jinping's governments in Beijing cut immediate military risk through tourism, trade, and official contacts, even though sovereignty stayed unresolved.

  5. 2016-2024 Pressure, identity drift, and U.S.-China fusion

    From 2016 to 2024, Tsai Ing-wen's rejection of Beijing's preferred political formula, Xi Jinping's harder line, Donald Trump's 2018 trade and technology turn, and later U.S. support under Joe Biden moved Taiwan from a managed dispute into the center of U.S.-China strategic competition.

  6. 2025-2026 Summit pause over a harder baseline

    From late 2025 to October 2026, Donald Trump and Xi Jinping used Busan and Beijing summits to cap parts of the tariff and export-control fight, but neither side changed the 1979 political formula, Taiwan's separate government, or the PLA's long-run preparation for coercive options.

Where today sits. On trend: October 2026 stays on the harder post-2016 path, after U.S. operations in the Taiwan Strait rose from 3 in 2015 to 9 in 2019. October 2026 still sits below the declared-crisis levels of 1954-55, 1958 and 1995-96 because the late-2025 and May 2026 record shows Busan and Beijing summit diplomacy rather than mobilization for immediate war. 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9

Trajectory and driving forces

Since Donald Trump's first term in 2017-2021, Washington has treated China less as an economic partner and more as a trade, technology, and military rival, and Taiwan has moved with that shift. Since Tsai Ing-wen took office in 2016 and Lai Ching-te kept the Democratic Progressive Party (DPP) in power in 2024, Xi Jinping's government has relied more on military pressure, diplomatic isolation, and political messaging than on cross-Strait accommodation. By October 2026, the late-2025 Busan bargains and Trump's May 2026 Beijing visit have produced a pause in tariff escalation, but none of the cited 2026 assessments show a deal on sovereignty, arms support, or alliance planning, so the medium-term direction still points to a harder deterrence contest rather than a settled modus vivendi. 1 2 3 4 5 6 7 8 9

  • CCP legitimacy and Xi's calendar. Xi Jinping has linked unification with Taiwan to the CCP's national project and to the 2049 centenary horizon, so Beijing has an incentive to keep raising pressure even if the PLA does not receive an order for war in 2026 or 2027.
  • U.S. legal ambiguity plus bipartisan competition. The 1979 Taiwan Relations Act, the three communiqués, and Reagan's Six Assurances let Washington arm Taiwan without a defense treaty, and the 2018-onward competition with China gives Congress, the Pentagon, and allied capitals reasons to strengthen that support.
  • Taiwan democracy and identity. Taiwan's democratization after the 1990s and the DPP victories of 2016 and 2024 make any political formula from Beijing less acceptable to many voters in Taipei, which pushes Beijing away from persuasion and toward pressure.
  • Military balance and allied planning. PLA modernization, U.S. Indo-Pacific Command planning, AUKUS, and Japan-South Korea trilateral exercises mean that every move around Taiwan now carries alliance and escalation consequences across Okinawa, Guam, and the Philippine Sea.
  • Semiconductor and energy exposure. TSMC's place in advanced chips and Taiwan's dependence on imported energy make blockade or quarantine threats more usable for Beijing and more costly for the United States, Japan, and Europe than a narrow island dispute was in 1996.

Turning points

  1. 1949

    PRC founded; ROC government retreats to Taiwan Mao Zedong's victory in October 1949 and Chiang Kai-shek's retreat to Taipei created rival governments claiming China. That split turned Taiwan from a civil-war refuge into a standing U.S.-China security question once the Korean War began in 1950. 1

  2. 1972

    Shanghai Communiqué opens U.S.-PRC normalization Richard Nixon's February 1972 trip to Beijing and the Shanghai Communiqué shifted Washington away from exclusive recognition of Taipei. The 1972 formula did not settle Taiwan's sovereignty, so it reduced one dispute while preserving the conditions for future ambiguity. 1 2

  3. 1979

    Washington recognizes Beijing and passes the Taiwan Relations Act Jimmy Carter's 1979 recognition of the PRC ended formal U.S. ties with Taipei, but the U.S. Congress answered with the Taiwan Relations Act. The 1979 settlement locked in the structure that still governs 2026: no embassy, arms support, and no agreed final status. 1 2 3

  4. 1995

    1995-1996 Taiwan Strait Crisis Lee Teng-hui's 1995 U.S. visit and Taiwan's March 1996 election led Beijing to use missile tests and military exercises near Taiwan. Bill Clinton's deployment of two carrier groups showed that Washington would still intervene to prevent coercion from deciding Taiwan's politics. 1 2

  5. 2016

    Tsai Ing-wen's election ends the previous cross-Strait political understanding Tsai Ing-wen's May 2016 inauguration left Beijing without acceptance of the 1992 Consensus, which Beijing had treated as the floor for official dealings. From 2016 onward, Beijing increased military, diplomatic, and economic pressure instead of returning to the 2008-2015 pattern. 1 2

  6. 2018

    Trump's tariff turn and the Taiwan Travel Act fuse Taiwan to wider rivalry Donald Trump's 2018 tariffs, export-control push, and security framing moved China policy onto a competition track, while the Taiwan Travel Act expanded the political space for higher-level U.S.-Taiwan contact. After 2018, Taiwan ceased to be a partly separate file and became part of a wider U.S.-China contest over trade, technology, and military position. 1 2

  7. 2025-11

    Busan summit pauses parts of the trade and export-control fight Donald Trump and Xi Jinping used the November 2025 meeting in Busan to reduce some tariffs, resume Chinese purchases of U.S. agricultural goods, and temporarily suspend some export-control and rare-earth measures. The Busan package lowered near-term economic pressure, but the cited assessments state that Taiwan's underlying political dispute was not resolved there. 1 2

  8. 2026-05-13

    Trump's Beijing visit confirms leader-level stabilization without a Taiwan settlement Donald Trump's 13 May 2026 state visit to Beijing showed that both capitals still value top-level crisis management. The same 2026 reporting describes a framework of fragile coexistence rather than any bargain that changes Beijing's unification goal, Washington's Taiwan framework, or Taipei's self-government. 1 2

Current phase

As of October 2026, Washington, Beijing, and Taipei are in a phase of fragile summit stabilization over a militarized and technologized rivalry. 1 2 3

What's at stake.

  • Taiwan: Its de facto independence and the reliability of US arms deliveries.
  • China: Its unification claim and access to Western technology.
  • United States: Its credibility with allies and its lead in advanced chips.

Historical analogue: Third Taiwan Strait Crisis, 1995-1996

Why comparable. The 1995-1996 crisis fits because Jiang Zemin used missile tests and exercises to shape Taiwan's first direct presidential election in March 1996, while Bill Clinton used two carrier battle groups to restore deterrence.

Where it breaks. The 2026 setting differs because Xi Jinping has a far larger missile, cyber, and maritime tool kit than Jiang Zemin had in 1996, and Taiwan now sits at the center of the global advanced-chip supply chain.

What happened then. In 1995-1996, Beijing fired missiles near Taiwan and Washington deployed two carrier groups into nearby waters. 1 2 3

Links to other storylines

  • Trade wars, tariffs and supply chains: The late-2025 Busan tariff and soybean bargains gave Donald Trump and Xi Jinping a diplomatic floor, so any collapse in tariff or rare-earth arrangements in 2027 would remove one of the few channels that both capitals still value.
  • AI race, compute and chip export controls: TSMC and U.S. export-control policy bind Taiwan to the chip contest, because Washington treats advanced semiconductors as military-relevant technology and Beijing treats access to compute as a growth and security requirement in 2026.
  • Indo-Pacific flashpoints: AUKUS, U.S.-Japan planning, and U.S.-Japan-South Korea exercises matter because any Taiwan contingency would pull Australia, Japan, Guam, and first-island-chain sea lanes into the same theater.
  • Energy and commodities: Taiwan's dependence on imported energy gives Beijing non-kinetic leverage in any 2027 quarantine episode, and it also makes stockpiles, LNG shipping, and port resilience part of deterrence.

Counterview. The harder-trajectory reading may overrate Xi Jinping's willingness to trade growth for coercion in 2027. The November 2025 Busan deals, the May 2026 Beijing visit, and the expert divisions reported by Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) at the end of 2025 support a narrower possibility: Donald Trump may keep treating Taiwan as one item inside a larger bargain on tariffs, soybeans, rare earths, and export controls, while Xi Jinping may judge that pressure short of war already serves Beijing's interests at lower cost than invasion. Lai Ching-te and Taiwan's opposition parties also have reasons in 2026 to avoid any legal move toward independence, which leaves the old deterrence formula battered but still operating.
