Cyber conflict and information operations

Strategic arc

Where it stands in the longer history inflection

  1. 1980s-2007 State experimentation

    KGB-linked espionage cases in the United States in the 1980s and the 2007 Estonia denial-of-service campaign showed that states could use networks for theft and political coercion before malware had large physical effects.

  2. 2008-2012 Sabotage enters industry

    Russia's 2008 Georgia campaign, the 2010 Stuxnet operation against Iran's Natanz facility and the 2012 Shamoon attack on Saudi Aramco moved cyber conflict from website disruption to industrial sabotage and economic damage, including damage to 20 percent of Iranian centrifuges and more than 35,000 Saudi computers.

  3. 2013-2017 Ukraine becomes the proving ground

    Russia's 2013 Operation Armageddon, the 2014 Crimea communications attacks, the 2015 and 2016 Ukraine power-grid disruptions and the June 2017 supply-chain attack from Ukraine showed how espionage, operational technology (OT) attacks and cross-border spillover could sit inside one campaign.

  4. 2018-2021 Criminal and national-security layers converge

    From 2018 to the 2021 Colonial Pipeline disruption in the United States, ransomware, supply-chain exploitation and edge-device abuse turned private companies, hospitals and fuel networks into national-security assets rather than only commercial targets.

  5. 2022-2024 War-time integration, limited decisiveness

    Russia's February 2022 invasion of Ukraine confirmed that wipers, satellite disruption and information operations can support conventional war, but Center for Strategic and International Studies (CSIS) found that cyber effects still did not replace artillery, missiles or logistics as the main determinants of battlefield outcome.

  6. 2025-2026 Persistent access and artificial intelligence (AI)-assisted deception

    The February 2025 theft of $1.5 billion from ByBit, late-March 2026 tracking of about 5,800 Iran-linked attacks, and September 2026 cases involving Anthropic targets, United Kingdom Security Service (MI5) warnings and South Africa's Air Traffic and Navigation Services (ATNS) show a shift toward long-dwell access, AI-enabled impersonation, state-linked theft and selective OT pressure.

Where today sits. Inflection: October 2026 sits beyond the 2010-2017 pattern of occasional high-visibility sabotage such as Stuxnet in 2010 and the June 2017 supply-chain attack from Ukraine, because CSIS, Google Cloud and Recorded Future describe steady pressure through telecom footholds, AI-targeted phishing, crypto theft and influence operations in 2025-2026. October 2026 also fits the February 2022 lesson from Ukraine that cyber operations buy access, intelligence and decision friction more often than battlefield victory, while September 2026 cases involving Anthropic targets, United Kingdom (UK) academia and South Africa’s ATNS show a wider target set. Trajectory and driving forces

Since 2024, China-linked operators, Russia-linked operators, Iran-linked operators and North Korean operators have pushed cyber competition toward persistent access, selective OT disruption, large-scale theft and continuous narrative pressure rather than rare one-off shocks. CSIS recorded a $1.5 billion North Korean theft from ByBit in February 2025, while September 2026 cases included China-linked phishing against AI policy experts, MI5 accusations about more than 100 UK academics and ransomware-linked malware in South Africa's ATNS OT network. The mechanism is strategic patience: Beijing, Moscow, Tehran and Pyongyang can hold access for months or years, activate proxies during crises and use AI to cut the cost of reconnaissance, impersonation and translation, while the United Nations and national legal systems still offer few hard thresholds below armed conflict. [8] [9] [10] [11] [12] [13] [14] [15] [16]

  • Pre-positioning for geopolitical crises. Beijing treats access in telecoms, ports, energy and government networks as a wartime reserve, because Google Cloud, EclecticIQ and U.S. reporting each describe China-linked operators seeking months- or years-long dwell time that could be activated during a Taiwan crisis; the main restraint for Beijing is that activation would expose tooling and invite sanctions or counter-cyber action.
  • Proxy and deniable retaliation. Moscow, Tehran and Israel-linked fronts use proxies, leak channels and deniable fronts because Russia, Iran and Israel can raise costs for rivals without crossing declared military red lines; the restraint for all three is that a visible hit on hospitals, air traffic or water systems can still trigger public attribution and financial retaliation.
  • AI lowers the cost of persuasion and impersonation. OpenAI website incidents in September 2026, China-linked phishing against AI policy experts in September 2026 and U.S. Air Force Institute warnings for 2026 all show that generative AI cuts the labor needed for reconnaissance, writing, translation and persona management; high-end compromise still requires stolen data, infrastructure and patient testing.
  • State finance and cybercrime are converging. Pyongyang's February 2025 ByBit theft showed that sanctions pressure can push North Korea to use cyber theft as a revenue line, while ransomware and leak markets give state-linked actors cover and talent pools; laundering at scale still depends on exchanges, mixers and foreign enforcement gaps.
  • Law and norms lag operations. The 2025 United Nations (UN) OEWG outcome and the 2026 Lieber Institute review show that states still reaffirm existing international law without settling whether data corruption, denial of access or loss of functionality counts as an attack, so operators keep room for coercive activity below clear armed-conflict thresholds; a mass-casualty hit on air traffic or hospitals would put that ambiguity under political pressure.

Turning points

  1. 2007

    Estonia denial-of-service campaign The 2007 attacks on Estonian government and banking services showed that politically motivated network disruption could punish a state below the threshold of armed attack and pushed North Atlantic Treaty Organization (NATO) members to treat civilian connectivity as a security issue. [2]

  2. 2010

    Stuxnet damages Iran's Natanz facility The reported U.S.-Israeli Stuxnet operation destroyed 20 percent of Iranian centrifuges and proved that malware could produce physical industrial effects rather than only steal data or deface websites. [3]

  3. 2012

    Shamoon hits Saudi Aramco The 2012 Shamoon attack destroyed more than 35,000 Saudi Aramco computers and widened the target set from secret military systems to commercial energy firms and national economic infrastructure. [4]

  4. 2014-05

    Crimea seizure and Ukraine election interference Russia used communications attacks in Crimea and targeted Ukraine's May 2014 presidential election, creating a model in which territorial action, network disruption and narrative operations reinforce one another. [5]

  5. 2015-12

    Ukraine power-grid disruption The 2015 and 2016 attacks on Ukraine's power system proved that OT disruption was no longer a laboratory problem and pushed utilities in Europe and North America to treat industrial control systems as a frontline security issue. [5] [6]

  6. 2017-06

    Ukraine supply-chain spillover The June 2017 malware campaign spread beyond Ukraine through software supply chains, showing that a state campaign aimed at one theater can impose costs on firms and governments worldwide within days. [5]

  7. 2022-02

    Russia's full-scale invasion tests cyber-war claims Russia paired the February 2022 invasion with wipers and network attacks, but CSIS judged that the anticipated cyber thunder run did not arrive, pushing analysts to treat cyber as a support arm for espionage, deception and temporary disruption rather than a war-winning substitute. [2] [5]

  8. 2025-02

    North Korea's ByBit theft The February 2025 theft of $1.5 billion in Ethereum from Dubai-based ByBit showed that state-linked cyber crime can finance a sanctioned government at a scale large enough to affect sanctions enforcement and exchange regulation. [8]

Current phase

October 2026 is a phase of persistent, below-war-threshold competition in which China, Russia, Iran, Israel-linked groups and North Korea mix long-dwell access, selective disruption, theft and influence operations across civilian networks. [9] [10] [11] [2] [8]

Links to other storylines

  • US-China rivalry and Taiwan: U.S. and allied concern about China-linked footholds in telecoms, ports and energy networks makes cyber access part of any Taiwan contingency, because Beijing can threaten delay and confusion in Guam, Japan or the continental United States before a first shot.
  • Middle East and the Iran axis: Iran-Israel confrontation keeps producing bank disruption, CCTV exploitation, denial-of-service activity and influence campaigns, and each round increases spillover risk for Gulf finance, shipping and diaspora communities.
  • AI race, compute and chip export controls: China-linked phishing against AI policy experts and concern about model-weight theft tie cyber espionage to export controls, datacenter security and talent screening at Anthropic, OpenAI and chip suppliers.
  • Trade wars, tariffs and supply chains: U.S. router restrictions, ENISA debates over CVE governance and supply-chain trust reviews turn cyber exposure into procurement policy, raising costs for carriers, cloud firms and manufacturers in Europe and Asia.

Counterview. CSIS work on the Russia-Ukraine war supports a narrower reading: Russia entered February 2022 with years of access in Ukraine, yet artillery, missiles and manpower still mattered more than malware for territorial gains. Ukraine since 2022 also suggests that many pre-positioning claims never translate into activation, because once Beijing, Moscow or Tehran burn access they expose tools, invite sanctions and lose espionage channels built over years. Corporate forecasts about AI-enabled social engineering may also overstate operational payoff, because banks, ministries and platforms in 2026 already use multifactor controls, takedowns and fraud analytics that can blunt mass impersonation.

Daily timeline

  1. 2026-11-01 ■ steady

    Binance said it had replaced 90% of the $6.3 billion stolen from its platform on Oct. 29 and began restoring withdrawals on Nov. 1, while France 24 put the share at 95%. The FBI attributed the theft to North Korea's TraderTraitor group, which was behind last year's $1.5 billion Bybit theft, about a quarter of this one (calculated). [17] [18]

    Why: Binance absorbs the loss from more than $6.34 billion in company funds to keep users and prevent a run on withdrawals. The FBI says the hackers are converting stolen assets into Ethereum before moving them through decentralised finance and swap services. Binance users are made whole on bitcoin, Ethereum, Tether and seven other assets, and the company carries the loss. Pyongyang gains hard currency outside the reach of sanctions to the extent the laundering succeeds.

  2. 2026-10-31 ▲ escalating

    Binance, the largest cryptocurrency exchange, said hackers stole more than $6 billion in under 30 minutes late on Oct. 29. TRM Labs and Chainalysis said with high confidence that North Korea's Lazarus Group, also known as TraderTraitor, is the likely culprit. Binance halted withdrawals and said it would cover the losses. [19]

    Why: Sanctions cut North Korea off from most legal revenue, which makes theft of liquid digital assets attractive. Large exchanges hold enough in hot wallets to make a single breach worth billions. Pyongyang may gain hard currency outside the sanctions system if the funds are laundered. Binance absorbs the loss, and crypto prices fell on the news.

  3. 2026-10-28 ▲ escalating

    The Federal Election Commission's electronic filing system has been offline since about 10 p.m. ET on Oct. 26 after reported hacking attempts, according to an email seen by Reuters. Campaigns cannot file 24-hour and 48-hour spending reports electronically. [20] [21] [22]

    Why: The FEC shut down web-facing services after the attempts. The agency has not said who was responsible or whether the intrusion succeeded. Voters and journalists lose timely data on late spending in close races such as the Texas Senate contest. Separately, Russia-linked AI videos are spreading false claims about Democratic Senate candidate Sherrod Brown, according to inkl.
