Thread · Electoral anger meets institutions

Voters in Brazil and Serbia test how far anger at incumbents can change who governs

Current direction. Incumbents and long-ruling parties are on the defensive across these contests. Brazil's runoff shows an incumbent can still win, while Serbia shows a protest movement can now take power through the ballot box.

Read the full thread analysis

Where it comes from. Since 2024, voters across many democracies have punished incumbents over prices, crime, weak services and corruption. Brazil's runoff and Serbia's snap vote are the latest tests of whether that anger removes leaders or only shakes them.

What is driving it underneath. Cost of living, crime and corruption sit underneath both contests. In Brazil, Quaest polling found violence, corruption and the economy were the top concerns. In Serbia, a deadly station canopy collapse in 2024 turned anger at graft into a mass movement.

Who is driving it, and toward what.

  • Luiz Inácio Lula da Silva
    Aim: Lula sought a fourth term to keep Brazil's left in power and to change how the country is run, as he said in his victory speech.
    Evidence: He won the runoff with 51.79% to Flávio Bolsonaro's 48.21% after narrowly losing the first round. E1 E7
  • Flávio Bolsonaro
    Aim: The senator sought to return his family's right-wing movement to the presidency, and he now asks for the result to be annulled.
    Evidence: He called the result impossible to concede and asked for annulment soon after the electoral court confirmed Lula's win. E1 E7
  • Student List
    Aim: The student-led movement set out to root out corruption, depoliticise public institutions and restore citizens' rights.
    Evidence: A sample count gave it 135 to 140 of 250 seats, and Aleksandar Vucic conceded defeat. E26 G22
  • Aleksandar Vucic
    Aim: Vucic called a snap vote to renew his mandate after almost two years of protests against him.
    Evidence: He conceded after his list was projected at 105 seats on the highest turnout in more than 30 years. E26

What's at stake.

  • Brazil's left and right: Lula's camp gains four more years in power, while the Bolsonaro movement loses the presidency and decides whether to accept the count.
  • Serbian voters: Voters who backed the Student List want graft investigated and public institutions freed from party control.
  • United States: Washington loses an ally it had backed in Brazil, according to the Financial Times account of the result.

The tensions.

  • Popular anger vs institutional checks: Voters punish leaders at the ballot box, while electoral courts and certification decide whether losers accept the outcome.
  • Protest energy vs governing capacity: Serbia's Student List campaigned on corruption and education and has said little about its foreign policy, which it must now define in office.

What we don't know. Today's sources do not detail the legal basis for Bolsonaro's annulment request. Serbia's final official seat count is not yet published in today's sources.

Competing readings. Lula's supporters and European leaders call his win a defence of democracy, while Bolsonaro's camp disputes the count and argues the result does not reflect the country's mood. Serbia's opposition sees an end to state capture, while Vucic had described the protesters as blockaders seeking to destabilise the country.

How today's events connect.

  • Lula won because voters swung back in the runoff after a first-round defeat, and his victory affects Washington's push for greater influence in Latin America, as the Financial Times reports.
  • Vucic lost because a protest movement born of the Novi Sad disaster turned anger at corruption into votes, and the result affects how Serbia's state institutions are run after more than a decade of his rule.
  • The US midterm campaign is fought on the same cost-of-living anger because prices have risen 5.4% in Trump's second term, and that anger affects the Michigan Senate race that helps decide control of the chamber.

Counter-forces.

  • Flávio Bolsonaro
    Why it resists: His call to annul the vote challenges the electoral court's result and keeps his supporters mobilised.
  • Serbian Progressive Party
    Why it resists: Vucic's party still holds a large bloc of about 105 seats in the 250-member parliament on the sample count.

What's unresolved. It is unresolved whether Bolsonaro's annulment request gains any legal traction in Brazil's courts. It is also unresolved what foreign-policy line Serbia's Student List takes on the EU and Russia. E1 E7 E26 E14
