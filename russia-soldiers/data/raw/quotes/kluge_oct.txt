 The perils of privatized coercion - by Janis Kluge Russianomics Subscribe Sign in The perils of privatized coercion Russian regions and municipalities are increasingly turning to private recruitment agents to fulfil their quotas. This is leading to more aggressive and often criminal recruitment methods. Janis Kluge Oct 08, 2026 27 3 4 Share In late September, the prosecutor for Nizhny Tagil’s Leninsky district appeared before the budget commission of the City Duma with an unusual plea: He asked the commission to stop municipal payouts to recruitment intermediaries. In Nizhny Tagil, a recruitment agent receives 400,000 rubles ($4,500) for each new contract soldier he manages to recruit. The prosecutor lamented that criminal gangs were getting involved, with corrupt police officers and military recruitment officers participating in their schemes. Because of the huge payouts to recruiters, criminal activity in the area was increasing “exponentially”. However, the first deputy mayor of Nizhny Tagil contradicted the prosecutor: “It helps us to fulfil our tasks.” As long as these payouts remain legal, he said, the city would continue to use them. In 2025, agents in Nizhny Tagil delivered 580 men to recruitment offices, and 260 million rubles were paid out in return, according to the prosecutor. For 2026, he cited budget documents that provide for payouts for another 330 contracts. At least some of these financial flows ended up in the hands of organized crime. The case of Nizhny Tagil shows how bonuses offered to intermediaries can be a potent but dangerous recruitment instrument in the context of Russia’s corrupt capitalism. Agents are paid per contract signed, and it is up to them where they find men and how they convince them. And that “convincing” may also involve violence, as another case from Sverdlovsk Oblast demonstrates: Two criminals used violence to force their victims to sign contracts, taking their bonus payments afterwards. To some degree, coercive mobilization is already happening. It is not a centralized policy of the Kremlin, but rather the unintended result of delegating recruitment to lower levels of the hierarchy while maximizing pressure on governors and mayors to fulfil their quotas. Cities and regions do not care about the specifics of recruitment as long as they meet their targets. But over time, relying on (or at least tolerating) organized crime for recruitment may become dangerous. Privatizing coercion and violence creates more problems down the road, which is what the prosecutor in Nizhny Tagil is warning about. There is a certain parallel with Russia’s PMC Wagner fiasco of 2023, when the Kremlin outsourced part of its war effort to an entity it did not fully control. Criminal gangs conducting coercive recruitment for money are nowhere near as dangerous as Prigozhin’s “March on Moscow”, but the Kremlin may be wary of repeating the same mistakes. One day, the Russian government could decide to end its decentralized and (formally) voluntary recruitment system and switch to mobilization instead, for several reasons. Obviously, declining recruitment numbers and the insufficient fitness of recruited men would be at the top of the list. By contrast, recruitment costs alone are currently not high enough to push the Kremlin towards something as radical as another round of mobilization. But an underappreciated reason for another round of mobilization could be concerns in the Kremlin that the decentralized (and to some degree chaotic) recruitment campaign is getting out of hand. The biggest strength of the decentralized system - relying on the ruthlessness and ingenuity of lower-level Russian bureaucrats - could become its weakness. Increasingly desperate recruitment policies in underperforming districts and regions, uncontrolled intermediaries and “privatized coercion” by criminal gangs pose risks that may appear greater to Putin than the risks of mobilization. That is why he could opt for mobilization eventually: to reestablish control over the process. September recruitment data The pace of recruitment remains stable at around 1,000 per day, but is lower than in 2025. Based on Russian regional budget spending, recruitment reached 29,900 (last year: 31,900) in September, bringing the total to 263,000 new contract soldiers over the first three quarters (last year: 292,000). As the end of the fiscal year approaches, budget pressures are increasingly apparent in many regions of Russia. As in the previous year, the average size of recruitment bonuses is stagnating at the beginning of the fourth quarter, after increasing in the first half of the year. There are reports from several regions of cuts to spending in other areas (public sector wages, road repairs, investments) to free up funds for recruitment bonuses.
