Where Russia's shadow-fleet tankers go

Russia moves much of its seaborne oil on ageing tankers with opaque owners, frequent name and flag changes and patchy insurance, often called the shadow fleet. This page follows about 720 of these tankers using public ship-tracking data from Global Fishing Watch, and shows how the fleet keeps oil moving: where it loads, where it waits, how ships change identity, who runs them and when they go dark. Step through the story below, or switch to Explore to use the map yourself.

What the data shows

Default view: trips from Russian loading ports (line width = number of trips) and transfer areas (circle size = loitering). Lines link start and end points only; they are not the routes sailed.

Moving ships travel in straight lines between recorded positions (port calls, loitering and transponder-gap points), so a path may cross land. Radar detections cover selected chokepoints only.

The shadow fleet in brief
Why it is called a shadow fleet

Most of these ships are old tankers, often 15 to 20 years or more, bought cheaply as mainstream owners retired them. Each one usually sits in its own shell company registered in places such as the Marshall Islands, Mauritius or the United Arab Emirates, so the real owner is hard to see.

The ships change names and flags often. When a registry drops a tanker, it moves to another flag of convenience, and some have sailed under flags from registries that had already removed them. Many switch off or falsify their AIS transponders (the radio beacons ships use to report their position), and many lack insurance from the established Western clubs.

How it operates

Cargo is often moved between tankers at sea in ship-to-ship transfers, which blur where oil came from. Ships wait or drift for long periods in open water before and after these transfers, which shows up in tracking data as loitering.

Transponders tend to go silent near loading terminals and transfer areas, so a ship can load or swap cargo without a visible record. Paperwork such as cargo origin and insurance certificates is then reissued to present the oil as legitimate.

What it carries and what risks it brings

The fleet carries Russian crude oil and refined products to buyers outside the Western price-cap system, keeping export revenue flowing to Moscow despite Western restrictions.

Its age and weak insurance raise the risk of a spill that no insurer would cover. A tanker lost power and drifted off Germany's Rügen island in January 2025 carrying about 100,000 tonnes of oil. On 25 December 2024 the tanker Eagle S, which had loaded at a Russian port, dragged its anchor across the Gulf of Finland and cut the Estlink 2 power cable and four data cables. Finnish prosecutors charged its officers with negligence, not deliberate sabotage, and whether the damage was intended remains disputed. A Helsinki court dismissed the case in October 2025 for lack of Finnish jurisdiction. The Court of Appeal reversed that in August 2026 and sent the case back for trial, and that decision can still be appealed.

Where it goes

Western cargoes load at Primorsk and Ust-Luga on the Baltic, Novorossiysk on the Black Sea, and Murmansk in the Arctic. Pacific cargoes load at Kozmino near Nakhodka.

Baltic ships pass through the Gulf of Finland and the Danish straits. Transfers cluster off southern Greece, near Ceuta at the Strait of Gibraltar, in the Gulf of Oman and east of Singapore off Malaysia. The main buyers are India, China and Turkey.
